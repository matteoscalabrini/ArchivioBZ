# A morning in the Monument to Victory, 14 November 2026: five printed pieces shown in BZ '18–'45, cut square and traced

Research date: 14 November 2026. On that morning the author photographed the permanent exhibition *BZ '18–'45* in the crypt of the Monument to Victory. Five flat printed pieces hang there as reproductions; their photographs were cut to the sheet and set square (§2), and each piece was then traced: what it is, who made and printed it, where an original is held, and what happened on the occasion it was made for. §1 and §2 and the passages headed "Added by hand" are the lead researcher's; §3 to §7 were researched by three Opus researchers working in parallel and were checked, corrected and joined here. Downloads and photographs: `files/17_monument_visit/`, listed in its `manifest.md`. Rows 17.01 to 17.30 of the library; three further things joined rows 5.01, 5.02 and 5.12. On the same day, at the author's word ("catalog and include all picture i took. If some make sense to merge to exhisting entries, do"), every photograph of the morning was catalogued: §8.

## 1. The visit: what was photographed

### Takeaway
On the morning of 14 November 2026 (12:36–13:04 by the camera's clock) the author photographed the permanent exhibition *BZ '18–'45. One monument, one city, two dictatorships* in the crypt of the Monument to Victory, the monument from outside, and the Roman column in the park behind it: 49 photographs, kept in `Foto Vittoria/` at the root of the repository. ## 2. How the five pictures were cut

### Takeaway
Each piece was photographed from below and slightly to one side. The pictures in `images/*_visit.jpg` are those photographs cut to the sheet and set square with `library/tools/rectify.py`; the cuts are described in `rectify.json` beside this folder's manifest and can be made again from the photographs in `photos/`. Nothing was retouched: shadows of the frames and patches of sun are still on the sheets.

### Cited Findings
- Proportions were not assumed. A rectangle seen in perspective fixes its own width-to-height ratio once the lens is known; with the focal length from each photograph's EXIF the five sheets come out at 0.623 (*Esposizione*), 0.699 (Steinberger), 0.683 (*Settimana*), 0.746 (*Illustrazione*) and 0.703 (*Colonna Romana*), and in every case the two sides of the reconstructed rectangle meet at 90.0° to 90.3°. The *Esposizione* sheet measured again on the wide photograph, with another lens setting, gives 0.620. [V] — output of `rectify.py cut`
- Every sheet's edges sag inward between its corners, by 0.1 to 0.5 % of the edge's length. That is the prints, not the lens: on `photos/IMG_1769.JPG` a steel frame edge about 1,000 px from the picture's centre and 2,500 px long is straight to about 3 px, where a lens distortion fitted to the sheets' edges would have bent it by about 20 px. Each edge was pulled straight by the amount read off the tool's check pictures (the `bow` values in `rectify.json`), and 2 to 4 px of blurred rim were trimmed. [V]
- The lower right margin of the *Esposizione* sheet lies outside the close photograph (a wedge at most 38 px high, 23,471 px, 0.3 % of the picture). It was taken from the wide photograph `photos/IMG_1769.JPG`, colour-matched and feathered. [V]
- Resolution: the cut pictures are 1,500 to 2,350 px wide, limited by how much of each 12-megapixel photograph the sheet filled. [V]

### Gaps
- Uneven light is untouched: a shadow band along the top of the Steinberger and *Esposizione* sheets, a diagonal shadow and a patch of sun on the *Colonna Romana* poster. A second visit with the sheets in even light, or a scan from a holding archive, would do better than any correction.
- The frames are deep and were photographed from below; whether a few millimetres of each sheet's lower edge are hidden behind a frame's lip cannot be told from the photographs. On all five the lower margin or border is visible.

## 3. "Iª ESPOSIZIONE NAZIONALE dell'ALTO ADIGE / 10 AGOSTO – BOLZANO – 10 SETTEMBRE / sotto l'Alto Patronato del Capo del Governo BENITO MUSSOLINI", 1929


### Takeaway
The poster advertised the first "national" exhibition held in Bolzano. It ran from 10 August to 10 September 1929. Mussolini decreed it on 27 March 1929 and accepted its honorary presidency. The Duchess of Aosta and the Duke of Bergamo opened it with the economy minister Martelli. Lacking one building, it spread over eight: the Regina Elena, Cairoli and Filippo Neri schools, the Franciscan gymnasium, the Balilla gym, the Liceo Carducci, the Sale Civiche and the Kurhaus in Gries. More than 800 firms and 28 public bodies from the "old provinces" took part, and it drew more than 100,000 visits. The rail fare cut, granted by the transport minister Ciano, was 50% (not 70%), and tickets bought outside the Tre Venezie were valid for ten days. The press framed the exhibition as one more "band" tying the new border province to the rest of the kingdom. At the close the prefect announced that Mussolini wanted such an exhibition in Bolzano every third year. That is why this one is the "prima". A "zweite Reichsausstellung des Oberetsch" was planned in July 1931, but no 1932 edition was found. The scaled-down successor was the 1933 week of §5. Salce holds the poster (inv. 17975, chromolithograph, 100 × 70 cm, printed by the Tipo-Lito Carlo Perego in Milan); its record, opened by hand, names no illustrator. The Salce sheet has a line that the copy in the crypt lacks, "Riduzioni ferroviarie del 50%", and it is squatter: the crypt shows another, smaller edition of the same drawing. The signature on that edition ("G·M"-like) is still unread. The press names Lenhart only as the designer of the exhibition's horse-show poster, not of this one.

### 4.2 The author: Ludwig Steinberger (Munich 1879 – Munich 1968), historian of Bavaria and Tyrol, toponymist

#### Takeaway
Ludwig Steinberger was born in Munich on 12 August 1879 and died there on 7 February 1968. A historian (his first book: *Die Jesuiten und die Friedensfrage … 1635–1650*, Herder, 1906) and later a place-name scholar of Bavaria and Tyrol, he held a non-chair extraordinary professorship of medieval and modern history at Munich from 1926 to 1933. He wrote on the Brenner and on Tyrolean names long before 1932. After the pamphlet he was dismissed (27 June 1933, per the BSB), emigrated to Tyrol in 1933, fled to Switzerland after the Anschluss, was stripped of German citizenship on 6 October 1941, returned in 1946 to a professorship of place- and field-name research, retired in 1950 and kept teaching. His papers are at the UB LMU.

### 4.3 What it argues, and the summer of 1932 it answers

#### Takeaway
The researcher read no text of the pamphlet. The foreword and the third and fourth parts were read afterwards on the Teßmann scans (§4.8, §4.9), and they bear out what follows. The two period descriptions agree: the *gerade Weg* advertisement says it proves "den Verrat Hitlers an Südtirol" with "umfangreichem Tatsachenmaterial". The Innsbruck *Der Südtiroler* (15 January 1933) summarises it in three steps: South Tyrol is core German land; the Italians' slogan "Italien bis zum Brenner"; the suffering since the Italian occupation, using Reut-Nicolussi's *Tirol unterm Beil*. A polemical last part attacks National Socialism's conduct on South Tyrol and charges the Innsbruck paper itself with "einem beneidenswerten Optimismus gegenüber Hitlers Verhalten in der Südtiroler Frage". That charge fits the record. In March 1932 *Der Südtiroler* had headlined "Hitler verzichtet keineswegs auf Südtirol!". In June 1932 Hitler testified in Munich, at the perjury trial of Werner Abel (who had sworn that Hitler took Italian money), that he recognised Italian sovereignty over South Tyrol. In July, on Hitler's application, a Munich court forbade a Social Democratic city councillor to show a picture poster on "Hitlers Verrat in Südtirol" or to say that Hitler had betrayed South Tyrol. The pamphlet appeared in the middle of that fight, a week before the election of 31 July 1932.

#### Inferences
- [INF] The title and picture state the thesis in one image: South Tyrol caught in a web spun jointly by Rome (the fascist spider) and Munich (the Nazi spider, Munich being the NSDAP's seat), and the question "Mit ihm das ganze deutsche Land?" warns that what Hitler did to South Tyrol for Mussolini's alliance he would do to all Germany. A thesis in the author's own words needs the text (§4.1 Gaps).
- [INF] Dated against events: after Hitler's 1926 renunciation and the 1928 poster "Adolf Hitler entlarvt!" (§4.6); after Hitler's testimony at the Abel trial (June 1932); in the same weeks as the July hearings on the Munich injunction against the SPD "Südtirolplakat" (8 and 26 July 1932; the poster itself and the injunction belong to the Bavarian election of 24 April 1932, §4.9); one week before the Reichstag election of 31 July 1932. A Catholic-liberal professor's 53 pages in Fraktur "mit umfangreichem Tatsachenmaterial" made the same charge the court had just forbidden the SPD to make in a picture poster, but in the form of history. The cover still uses a picture to make the point.
- [INF] The pamphlet attacked from the anti-Nazi side the Austrian nationalist hope, voiced by *Der Südtiroler* in March 1932, that Hitler would not give South Tyrol up. The Innsbruck review six months later praises the history and passes over the reproach.

### 4.9 The text itself, read on Teßmann digital, 14 November 2026

#### Takeaway
The pamphlet has a foreword by another hand and four headed parts. The first two (pp. 5–16) say that Tyrol on both sides of the Brenner is German and follow the Italian claim to the Brenner back to the sixteenth century. The third, "Deutsch-Südtirols Kreuzweg" (pp. 17–27), is a record of Italian and Fascist rule from 1919 on. The fourth, "Deutsch-Südtirol und deutsche „Nationale“" (pp. 27–53), is half the book: a file of dated press quotations against Hitler. Bolzano is in it by name: the attack on the fair of 24 April 1921, the seizure of school and town hall in 1922, the *Alpenzeitung*, an NSDAP group founded in an inn in the Bindergasse in January 1932, and a Nazi wreath at the Monument to Victory on 9 December 1931. The title turns a line of the Andreas Hofer song around. Steinberger does not say that Hitler took Italian money, and he says outright that Hitler had no hand in handing South Tyrol over; his charge is that Hitler has disowned it since 1922 to please Mussolini. The text was closed between 9 and 22 July 1932.

Read on screen at full size, nothing downloaded: scans 1–6, 14 and 18–57, that is the foreword, the opening of each part, parts three and four whole, the two advertisements and the back cover. Not read: pp. 6–12 and 14–16.

#### Inferences
- [INF] The title is the song's line turned round. The song mourns "Ganz Deutschland … in Schmach und Schmerz, mit ihm das Land Tirol"; the cover says "Deutsch-Südtirol in Schmerz und Schand!" and asks "Mit ihm das ganze deutsche Land?". In the song Tyrol shares Germany's shame; in 1932 Germany is asked whether it wants South Tyrol's. Steinberger quotes the line and does not explain his title in the pages read. The last words, "von der Etsch bis an den Belt", answer the cover in the same way with the anthem's line, against the "von der Sill bis an den Belt" that *Der Südtiroler* had put in the mouths of Hitler's followers (p. 41).
- [INF] Dating. The Lausanne agreement was signed on 9 July 1932 and the hearing of 22 July is still to come: the text was closed between the two, a week or two before *Der gerade Weg* announced it on 24 July (row 17.05).
- [INF] The method is a cuttings file, which fits the advertisement of a Munich press-cuttings archive on the last leaf. That the archive, the publisher and the foreword's author belong together is a guess; the address Ludwigstr. 17a is the lead.
- [INF] Why it hangs where it hangs. The crypt's station "The two fascisms shake hands" shows the Nazi delegation at the Monument on 28 October 1932. Three months before that day the pamphlet had already printed a Nazi wreath at the Monument with Fascist militia looking on, and a founding meeting in Bolzano under both flags. The cover was not chosen only for its spiders.
- [INF] For a text now shown inside the Monument, it has no word on the Monument as a building. Its Bolzano is a city of inns, a theatre, a town hall, a station, a printing house and a post office, where rule is felt in names, signs and papers.
- [INF] The word "optieren" on p. 48, seven years before the Option, is chance; it is noted because the Innsbruck project that shows the cover is a project on the Option.

## 5. "SETTIMANA DELL'ALTO ADIGE / RIDUZIONI FERROVIARIE DEL 70% / BOLZANO 22–31 OTTOBRE XI", Anton Hofer, Litografia G. Linke, October 1933


### Takeaway
The poster is by the Bozen designer Anton Hofer (1888–1979). It is dated 1933 and was printed by the Litografia G. Linke in Bolzano. Salce in Treviso holds an original (inv. 10835, 100.3 × 70.3 cm, chromolithograph), and the national catalogue serves a photograph of it. The date is October 1933: the "Oberetscher Woche" (the German press's name for the week) ran from 22 October 1933 and was then extended to 11 November. The poster was chosen in a competition for artists of the province. The Alpenzeitung printed the brief on 29 August 1933. It fixed the size at 1 × 0.70 m, asked for a ground colour plus three-colour printing, and named the motifs: one of the week's shows (the agricultural show, the crafts show or the fur-animal show) plus "an allegorical reference to the aims the regime pursues in industrial agriculture". It also fixed the words, Italian only, down to the 70% rail fare cut. The jury met in the Rathaus on 18 September 1933 and looked at 19 entries. It split the L. 1,000 prize between "A. Hofer" and "A. Pozzi", and "the design of Prof. Hofer" was chosen for printing. Lenhart, Merlet and Amonn got honourable mentions. So each sign on the poster answers one line of the brief: grapes for agriculture, pincers and hammer for crafts, the fox for the fur-animal show, and the fasces and the stylised Monument for the regime. The week's exhibition (agriculture, crafts, tourism, fur animals) was held in the Bürgersäle. An under-secretary of the Corporations ministry, Biagi, opened it with a wreath at the Monument. All tickets had been sold three days before the planned close. A second edition of the exhibition followed in October 1935, and Salce holds its poster as well.

### Inferences
- [INF] The date is October 1933; AZ 24.09 and 27.09.1933 give 22–31 October. One nuance: Year XI ended on 28 October 1933, so the printed "XI" was strictly true only for 22–28 October. The extension to 11 November (AZ 29.10.1933) ran wholly into Year XII. That is why de.wikipedia writes "Oktober–November 1933".
- [INF] The poster follows the competition brief point by point, and this explains every motif. The grapes stand for the agricultural show (wine and fruit), the pincers and hammer (Salce: "tenaglie; martello") for the crafts show, and the fox for the fur-animal show (silver foxes headed its prize list). The dark upright with two tricolour straps and a projecting blade (Salce: "fascio littorio") and the white block with three slots (Salce and Wikipedia: the Monument to Victory) are the "allegorical reference" to the regime. The jagged grey silhouette at right fits the tourism section, but no source names it.
- [INF] The colours also fit the brief: a dark ground plus three printed colours (green, light blue, red), with the white of the paper. The size fits as well: 1 × 0.70 m asked, 100.3 × 70.3 cm measured.
- [INF] The poster addressed Italians outside the province. Its words are Italian only (the brief allowed no German text), and its strongest line is the 70% fare cut. The German-speaking press called the event "Oberetscher Woche". No German edition of the poster has turned up, and the brief makes one unlikely.
- [INF] The Monument is reduced to a sign, a white block with three dark slots, read together with the fasces. This is the same move as the *Atesia Augusta* back cover (notes 16 §2), five years after the inauguration and in a commercial poster for a regional trade fair.
- [INF] Lenhart competed and got only an honourable mention, yet a year earlier he had drawn the fox for the first national fur-animal show (Leads). The jury included architects of both language groups (Marius Amonn, Francesco Rossi) and was chaired by the podestà Miori. The local German-speaking design world (Hofer, Amonn, Lenhart, Merlet) thus made the regime's Italian-language event graphics, which extends the "hinge figure" point of notes 01 Q5.

## 6. The cover of *L'Illustrazione Italiana*, Anno LXII, N. 35, 1 September 1935


### Takeaway
The cover photograph shows Mussolini's homage at the Monument to Victory on the afternoon of Monday 26 August 1935, the first day of his first stay in Bolzano (26–31 August 1935), which he made for the army's grand manoeuvres. Two local papers say that at the monument he was accompanied by the party secretary (Starace) and the prefect (Mastromattei), and one adds that the prefect wore the uniform of an Alpini major. That fits the two men on the cover: an Alpini officer at left and a blackshirt in white breeches at right. The identification is my inference and is not printed on the cover. The council of ministers did sit in Bolzano: it was convened for Wednesday 28 August at 4 p.m. in the prefecture (the "Palazzo del Governo"). No report I read has Mussolini making a speech in the city. The cover carries no photo credit, and no digitised copy of the issue was found. The Campari Soda advertisement carries a small two-line monogram that reads "CON / ALBI"; its designer is not identified.

## 7. The poster "Inaugurazione della Colonna Romana dedicata ai caduti dell'Alto Adige in terra d'Africa e in Ispagna", Bolzano, 5 June 1938


### Takeaway
The column was a gift of the city of Rome ("offerta dall'Urbe", "Geschenk der Stadt Rom an die Stadt Bolzano"). It was unveiled on Whit Sunday, 5 June 1938, in the park behind the Monument to Victory, in the presence of Crown Prince Umberto and Princess Maria José. The Duke of Pistoia, Under-Secretary for War Pariani, the prefect, the corps commander, the federal secretary and the podestà attended; Archbishop Celestino Endrici of Trento blessed it. The drape was pulled off by the father of the gold medallist Guido Presel, an airman killed near Bilbao. The families of "trentacinque atesini caduti" stood by the column. LPB gives four marble plaques: "Africa Orientale, Libia, Spagna" and «Agli alto atesini — Caduti per l'Impero — Roma riconoscente — A. XVI». The ceremony opened the day of the Festa dello Statuto parade and the "inauguration" of the Foro della Vittoria. It was part of a three-day Alpini festival under the crown prince's patronage: Merano, 4 June (a monument given by Milan); Bolzano, 5 June (the column given by Rome); Brunico, 6 June. Luce filmed the unveiling (B1319). Nothing found says the column is an ancient one, and its designer, the poster's designer "FR.", its printer and its holder are not found.

### Inferences
- [INF] Notes 15 §1 (Inferences) and the facts of row 15.17 guess that "the 1938 column" is "the colonnina del confine del Brennero" of B1312. That is superseded: the 1938 column is the Colonna Romana of 5 June. B1312 shows the roll call of 22 May 1938, two weeks earlier; the Luce date 01/06/1938 is the issue date. B1319 is the film of the unveiling.
- [INF] The poster's view cannot be stood in. It shows the east face with Dazzi's archer and puts the column on axis in front of it, whereas the column stands in the park on the west side. It also gives the column a capital and three steps that the column does not have. It is an emblem, not a view.
- [INF] Rome gave a column to Bolzano while Milan gave Merano its Alpini monument in the same week. The capital's gift of a "Roman" column renamed the dead of a German-speaking province as "atesini", and the roll call included an Andreas Hofer killed in Spain.
- [INF] "colonna romana" in 1938 can mean "a column from Rome" as much as "an antique column". No source read says it was excavated or ancient, and de.wikipedia's "von der römischen Klassik inspiriert" suggests a new piece.
- [INF] "FR." on the poster is a signature in the poster's own green ink, so it is the designer's or draughtsman's. No candidate is proven.

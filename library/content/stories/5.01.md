Four stone eagles stood on Ponte Druso for some forty years. They outlasted the regime that put them there by a generation, and nobody has yet found the document that ordered them down.

## A bridge opened on an anniversary

Ponte Druso was built for the state road authority to a design by the engineers Eugenio Miozzi and Gilberto Ricci. Work began in early 1930: the *Alpenzeitung* gives 9 January, the Comune's own guide gives March. It has three arches in reinforced concrete faced with rose porphyry and is 102.3 metres long. It cost three million lire, of which the Comune paid 800,000.

It opened on 28 October 1931, the ninth anniversary of the March on Rome. The *Alpenzeitung* of the next day shows how the date was used. The bridge was one item in a bundle: the municipal baths, the health-insurance building, a new radio transmitter, a power station in the Val d'Ega, an artillery barracks in Oltrisarco and a tram line were all opened the same day. At nine in the morning, in the rain, Prefect Marziali cut a tricolour ribbon. He was received by the "Erbauer Ing. Cav. Albini", a name none of the later accounts carry. Albini may have been the site engineer; that is a guess.

## What stood on the piers

The same newspaper page describes the sculpture, and it is the regime describing itself. The piers carry four helmets with a Roman shield, and swords pointing up and pointing down that "mark the beginning and the end of the war". The columns with the Roman eagles rest on globes carried by the lictor's bundle, on a base of laurel leaves carved in stone. From the roadway to the eagles the piers rise 8.10 metres.

The four eagles were carved in 1931 by Vittorio Morelli, a sculptor from Ancona. Two things about them are not settled. A local blog places them at the four corners of the bridge, while the Comune's guide places them on the four central piers; Mario Gabinio's photographs of 1932–35 are the way to check. And one Wikipedia caption credits the bridge's symbols to Alcide Ticò instead of Morelli.

Whether the eagles were Roman or fascist is not a real question. The *Alpenzeitung* names both in one sentence: eagles, on globes, on fasces.

## Why Drusus

The name points at the Roman general who took the Alps in 15 BC, and at *Pons Drusi*, a road station somewhere in the Bolzano basin. Two years after the opening the *Alpenzeitung* spelled it out: Drusus "conquered our Alpine territories with his legions, laid roads, founded settlements, carried Roman culture into the valleys". A Roman origin made an Italian Bolzano look like a return.

The idea went further than the bridge. Ettore Tolomei wanted the statue of Walther von der Vogelweide replaced by a monument to Drusus. Mussolini refused, counting the cost abroad. The claim itself is still in use: the city's tourism office writes that Bolzano "nasce probabilmente come statio militare romana nel 15 a.C. sotto il nome di 'Pons Drusi'".

## When the eagles came down

Not in 1945. A colour postcard of the 1960s shows them still on their piers. They went during a renovation of the bridge in the 1970s, and the sources do not agree on the year:

- 1972, in a blog that gives no source;
- the early 1970s, after the bridge's "lavatura", in the daily *Alto Adige*, whose 2013 headline "40 anni senza aquile" implies about 1973;
- the mid-1970s, in the Comune's guide: "furono eliminate tutte le parti con riferimenti ideologici";
- around 1970, during a road widening, in the German Wikipedia.

The *Alto Adige* gives the reason they never went back: "Troppo grandi, troppo fasciste e quindi ingombranti." Too big, too fascist, and so in the way. No decision by the road authority, the Comune or the Province has been found. It is possible that nobody decided to remove them and somebody decided not to put them back; that is an inference, not a finding.

In the 1990s the bridge was widened and given a new parapet in imitation of the old one. The eagles were not part of the imitation.

Sources: [*Alto Adige*, 5 September 2013](https://www.altoadige.it/cronaca/bolzano/ponte-druso-40-anni-senza-aquile-1.300394); [Verdorfer 2007](https://www.basrelief-bolzano.com/fileadmin/user_upload/pdfs/Gedenkorte/Bolzano.Percorso_tra_architettura_e_fascismo.pdf); [de.wikipedia, Drususbrücke](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drususbr%C3%BCcke_(Bozen)); [Gatti and Cacciaguerra 2007](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/77986).

## Where they went

For years they lay in the courtyard of the Museo Civico. Building work there in 2004 moved them to the municipal nursery at the end of via Rio Molino, where a reporter found them in 2013 "decapitate e mutilate", piled in the open.

On 6 December 2013, at eight in the morning, a crane truck carried the two best-preserved heads to the crypt of the Monument to Victory, for the documentation centre then being built inside it. Hannes Obermair, director of the city archive, supervised. The heads were only lightly brushed, "muschio compreso", moss included, so that visitors could see they were originals.

Sources: [*Alto Adige*, 4 September 2013](https://www.altoadige.it/cronaca/bolzano/le-aquile-di-ponte-druso-nel-museo-del-monumento-1.508168); [*Alto Adige*, 7 December 2013](https://www.altoadige.it/cronaca/bolzano/le-aquile-di-ponte-druso-in-volo-fino-alla-cripta-del-monumento-alla-vittoria-1.372917).

## Still open

- The year of the removal, and who decided it. The next place to look is the *Alto Adige* and *Dolomiten* of 1971 to 1976.
- Who Albini was.
- Whether the two heads are still on display: the documentation centre's 2025 guide does not mention them.
- Where the other two eagles are. Nothing has been found after 2013.
- What the inscriptions on the bridge-heads said. Gabinio's catalogue records a votive altar with a date.

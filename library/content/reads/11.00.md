# A colony's capital, 1890–1941

Asmara, on the high plateau of Eritrea, was the capital of Italy's oldest colony. This research studied it for a reason that has little to do with Africa. Bolzano under Fascism is often called a divided city, an Italian town built beside a German one. Asmara is what a divided colonial city of the same years looked like when the division was written into law, and it was filled with Italians by the same office that sent workers to Bolzano. It serves here as a control: a case to measure the other against.

## 1890–1896: farms that failed

Italy first tried to settle Eritrea with farmers. On 19 June 1890 the deputy Leopoldo Franchetti was appointed "commissario per la colonizzazione". [He set up](row:11.01) three experimental farms, at Asmara, Godofelassi and Gura, on "circa 4500 Ettari" of land declared state property, and brought peasant families "arruolati con atto notarile", enrolled by a notary's deed, with a promise of ownership. Fifteen to thirty families came, from Lombardy, Friuli and Sicily. A revolt in December 1894 and the defeat at Adwa on 1 March 1896 ended it. Decades later one or two families were left.

After that the colony's farming went to companies. The cotton scheme at Tessenei, begun in the 1920s, [was worked](row:11.20) by local and Sudanese sharecroppers; in 1938 the place had 1,850 inhabitants, 50 of them Italian. No farm-settlement agency of the kind created for Ethiopia in 1937 was found for Eritrea. The Fascist colony was settled through its city.

## 1908–1916: four zones

The division of that city is a generation older than Fascism. Two decrees of the governor Giuseppe Salvago Raggi, of 23 October and 19 December 1908, [split Asmara](row:11.17) into four zones. The first was reserved "esclusivamente per le abitazioni degli Italiani e dei Cittadini di Stati stranieri", for the homes of Italians and foreign citizens only. The second, round the market, was for mixed residence. The third was "riservata esclusivamente per le abitazioni dei sudditi coloniali e degli assimilati ad essi", for colonial subjects and those counted with them. The fourth took suburbs and industry.

The colony's chief engineer, Odoardo Cavagnari, drew the plan that followed between 1913 and 1916, and a decree of 1914 repeated the ban on natives living in the first zone. The body that later assessed Asmara for UNESCO [says](row:11.19) his plan "confirmed the racial segregation of the previous plan". The file Eritrea submitted [describes the purpose](row:11.18): to give Italians a city "reminiscent of home" and to "contain and control the native population, which consistently outnumbered the Italian community until the mid-1930s". Eritreans who owned property in the centre "were often forced to sell their land and move out".

## 1935–1937: the boom

Until 1935 Asmara was small: 3,000 to 4,000 Italians beside 12,000 to 20,000 Eritreans. The invasion of Ethiopia, launched from Eritrea in October 1935, turned it into the base of an army and of the road builders who followed.

From 1 April 1935 the municipality had a planning consultant, Guido Ferrazza, an architect born in Trentino. Italians were living in shacks, and in 1937 the governor ordered those inside the city pulled down within a month. That year Ferrazza's plan [was approved](row:11.17), and the planning journal reported its aim: "verrà accentuato il distacco della Città nazionale dal Quartiere indigeno", the detachment of the national city from the native quarter will be accentuated. At the edges there would be industrial zones and workers' quarters, where "ogni abitazione avrà un giardinetto ed un orticello", every dwelling will have a little garden and a vegetable plot. The historian who has read the ministry's files notes what the plan ignored: that the Eritreans were not far short of half the population and could not be shut into a single quarter.

A competition for the city's plan was held the same year and annulled. The first prize had gone to a group that included the governor.

Four "villaggi operai", workers' villages, were begun in 1937. The touring club's guide names them: "A. Mussolini, L. Razza, Col. De Cristoforis, Magg. Toselli". A newsreel of December 1937 [shows](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000028033/2/l-inizio-lavori-costruzione-due-nuovi-villaggi-operai-alla-presenza-del-governatore-eritrea-de-feo.html) the governor laying the first stone of two of them, followed by "la benedizione religiosa dei due villaggi". Three hundred small houses each were announced for the first two. Who designed and paid for them is not known.

## 1938–1939: a buffer and a green wall

In April 1938 the ministry sent a Roman architect, Vittorio Cafiero, who delivered 25 drawings on 20 February 1939. The ministry's planning council approved them that April.

Cafiero kept the mixed zone round the market as a buffer, so that, in the words of the UNESCO file, "the only contact of the whites with natives will be with the highest category of them, the commercial and industrial one". He moved a "Zona indigena" to the north-west, behind hills that were to be "trasformata in «barriera» verde", turned into a green barrier. The council asked for the dividing strip of green to be made wider. The governor of the day thought the native-quarter scheme "una esagerazione urbanistica", a planner's exaggeration, and reported at the same time: "Io sto attuando la bonifica del quartiere indigeno, abbattendo i tucul peggiori." I am carrying out the reclamation of the native quarter, knocking down the worst huts.

The guide of 1938 shows the line on the ground. It describes viale Milano as a "lunga arteria formicolante di indigeni, che separa nettam. la città italiana a S dal villaggio eritreo a N", a long artery swarming with natives that sharply divides the Italian city to the south from the Eritrean village to the north. It lists a covered market "per gli indigeni", a native court, a "Cimitero per gli Europei". It recommends a visit to the Eritrean village, which "può essere interessante per rendersi conto delle condizioni di vita degli eritrei", may be interesting for getting an idea of how the Eritreans live.

## 1937–1940: the laws on persons

Three statutes for the whole empire drew the line through private life. [A decree of 19 April 1937](row:11.07) punished an Italian citizen who kept a "relazione d'indole coniugale", a relation of a conjugal kind, with a colonial subject with one to five years in prison. [A law of 29 June 1939](row:11.10) "per la difesa del prestigio di razza", for the defence of racial prestige, defined the citizen as "il cittadino italiano metropolitano di razza ariana" and punished the one who "frequenti abitualmente luoghi aperti al pubblico riservati ai nativi", habitually frequents public places reserved for natives. It also forbade him to work for a native without the governor's written permission. [A law of 13 May 1940](row:11.11) ruled that the child of a citizen and a native "assume lo statuto del genitore nativo", takes the status of the native parent, and could not be recognised by the Italian one.

In the street the rules were made by ordinance. A historian [reports](row:11.12) ordinances that forbade whites to live in black districts and ordered separation in places of entertainment and on public transport; their texts have not been found. The UNESCO file states that "Eritreans were forbidden from using or crossing Viale Mussolini" and that after 1938 their movement "became tightly restricted", above all at night. Secondary school was closed to them.

The ban on living with Eritrean women was widely ignored. An association's figures of 1948, which the same historian calls questionable, put the women living with Italians at 1,150 in 1935 and 15,000 in 1940.

## Who the Italians were

By 1938 Italians were the majority. The guide prints "ab. 98 000 (di cui 53 000 Italiani)". [Another count](https://journals.openedition.org/diacronie/1919) gives 48,000 Italians against 36,000 Africans in early 1939, and a third 41,263 Italian men and 11,177 women. None rests on a census: "No proper census took place after 1931".

The figures by sex say what kind of majority it was. Most of these Italians were labourers and lorry drivers, recruited among the registered unemployed, on contracts that ran "a base semestrale", by the half year. Between October 1935 and June 1939, 201,440 workers left Italy for East Africa, and [207,983 went home](row:9.08), most of them because their contract was up; 2,368 died there. Firms paid late and imposed unpaid overtime.

They were sent by the Commissariato per le migrazioni e la colonizzazione interna, the regime's office for internal migration. Its commissioner weighed the contractors' requests against unemployment and gave each province a quota. Prefects divided it among the towns, and party, union and police screened the men. The Veneto sent most, 39,871 of the 189,737 [counted](row:11.21) up to September 1937. The region of Trento and Bolzano sent 1,415. The guide of 1938 lists a delegation of the commissariat among the offices of Asmara.

The Eritrean population grew as well, to between 36,000 and 45,000, drawn by the work. In Asmara "majority" meant outnumbered. Nobody was replaced.

The regime had its phrases for the place. Eritrea was the ["colonia primogenita"](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL3000051636/1/eritrea-colonia-primogenita-d-italia.html), the first-born colony. Asmara, the guide wrote, "si presenta come una graziosissima città italiana … tesa verso un avvenire veram. imperiale": presents itself as a most charming Italian city, reaching towards a truly imperial future. The name "piccola Roma", little Rome, which is often repeated today, was found in no document of the time.

# After 1941, and beside Bolzano

## 1941–2017: the city kept

British troops entered Asmara on 1 April 1941. A British booklet of 1944 described "a European city of broad boulevards, super-cinemas, super-Fascist buildings" beside a native area "crowded into a latrineless native quarter". The British repealed the laws of segregation and left much else in Italian hands: judges, tax officers, the municipality.

The evening walk along the main avenue, the *passeggiata*, [was taken over](row:11.18) by Eritreans "after 1941 when the entire city became open to them".

Eritrea was federated with Ethiopia in 1952, annexed in 1962 and became independent after a war that ended with the taking of Asmara on 24 May 1991. The Italian city was not pulled down in any of these changes. One of the first rules of the new municipality limited the height of buildings. The new state came to use the colonial city as a mark of what made Eritrea different from Ethiopia.

That use has been criticised. The scholar Mia Fuller [pointed out](row:11.15) in 2011 that the "historic perimeter" drawn by the first conservation project "corresponds alarmingly to what Italian planners included in the city's European and 'mixed' zones", so that it "excludes the same area Italian colonizers excluded under the rubric of quartiere indigeno".

The nomination to UNESCO answered her in part. The protected area includes the old native quarters, Arbate Asmera and Abbashawel, as [the assessors put it](row:11.19), "illustrating the exclusion and segregation suffered by the indigenous population". Asmara was inscribed on 8 July 2017 as "Asmara: A Modernist African City". The statement of its value names "colonial planning, based on functional and racial segregation principles".

In one square the paving had a mosaic of three fasces and the year "XVI" of the Fascist era. "The date has since been erased, but the fasces still exist."

## Beside Bolzano

The two cities share an office. The commissariat that set the quotas for Asmara had a desk in Bolzano from 1936–37, and both Italian majorities were made in the same five years.

They share men. Luigi Razza, the commissariat's founder, signed as minister the decree that took the land for Bolzano's industrial zone; he died in August 1935 [on the flight to Asmara](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000019726/2/i-funerali-luigi-razza-e-compagni-volo-periti-7-agosto-nell-incidente-aereo-cairo-e-l-asmara.html), and Asmara named a workers' village after him. Giuseppe Cobolli Gigli, minister of public works, inspected the roads of the empire and opened the bridge to Bolzano's workers' quarter. Ferrazza, who planned Asmara, came from Trentino and after the war advised the public-works office for Trentino-Alto Adige.

They share a house type. The workers' house with its vegetable plot appears in Asmara's plan in 1937, the year the Semirurali of Bolzano were first drawn.

And once they share a room. In August 1938 a newsreel [shows](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000031251/2/la-mostra-prodotti-impero.html) "la mostra dei prodotti dell'Impero", an exhibition of the products of the empire, held in Bolzano, with a map of East Africa and a statue of "l'infaticabile lavoratore italiano", the tireless Italian worker.

No document of the time compares the two places. A South Tyrolean journal [did](row:11.13) in 2008, setting the front of Asmara's party house beside Bolzano's Monument to Victory.

The differences are as plain, and they are the reason for the comparison. In Asmara the people already there were subjects, and the city zoned them. In Bolzano they were citizens, and no rule found barred a German speaker from the new city. Asmara's Italians were men on contract who mostly went home; Bolzano's were families in rented flats who stayed. Asmara's Italian population grew more than tenfold in five years, Bolzano's roughly doubled. After 1941 the Italian majority of Asmara dissolved, and the colonised took over the colonisers' city and kept it. In Bolzano the two groups went on living in the same town, and the argument about the Fascist buildings has been conducted by adding to them.

Asmara therefore tests a word. If "divided city" is used of Bolzano, this is the scale it is being measured on.

# The plan, 1925–1941

The Italian Bolzano was drawn before it was built, and the drawings can still be read. This text follows the town plan from the first competition to the law that approved it, and then looks at the other side of the same plan: what it removed, moved or disguised in the old Tyrolean town, and who else was building in those years.

## 1925–1927: one commune, one square renamed

The ground was prepared by decree. On 2 November 1925 the king [signed](row:14.05) one sentence: "Il comune di Gries è aggregato a quello di Bolzano." The commune of Gries is joined to that of Bolzano. Gries was the village across the river Talvera. An Austrian land survey of 1858 [shows](row:14.01) the plain between the two as fields. The new city would be built there.

A traveller's guide of 1927 [catches the moment](row:14.06) before it. Its plan has German street names and no new quarter. Bozen is "a town of 35,000 inhab." and, "since the end of 1926", capital of a province. The main square, it notes, was "called the 'Walther-Platz' up to 1925" and is now the Viktor-Emanuel-Platz; the monument to the medieval poet Walther von der Vogelweide in its middle "is the chief rallying-point of visitors".

## 1929–1933: a competition with three winners and no plan

In July 1929 the Comune announced a national competition for a plan. [By the account](row:14.20) of the historian who has read the ministry's files, the brief asked competitors to leave "intatto, o quasi, il vecchio centro", the old centre intact or nearly so, and to place "il nucleo più popoloso, più civile e più italiano", the most populous, most civil and most Italian nucleus, in the plain of Gries. The town was to grow from 35,000 inhabitants to 100,000.

Nine entries were shown in March 1930. The jury, on which the Roman architect Marcello Piacentini sat, declared three winners of equal rank: Adalberto Libera with Gino Pollini, Giovanni Muzio, and Ettore Sottsass. Three of the four were from Trentino, the next valley south. No plan came of it. In 1933 the city's chief engineer was told to make one out of the best entries. His plan gave the Monument to Victory no part in the layout of the town, and it had little success.

## 1934–1935: Piacentini

On 9 June 1934 a decree declared the zone "dal Monumento alla Vittoria fino alla piazza Druso" to be of public utility, which opened it to expropriation. That November the local paper made public that Piacentini had the commission. He took the engineer's plan as a base and laid over it what one account calls "uno spazioso e grandioso sistema di strade e di piazze", a spacious and grand system of streets and squares, with the monument as its centre.

How the plan worked on a single plot is shown by a sale of that year. When the insurance institute INA bought the site of the old slaughterhouse, it had to accept "un vincolo che deve consentire la visuale delle Dolomiti da Piazza della Vittoria", a restriction to keep the view of the Dolomites open from the square of the monument.

The city archive [shows the sheets](row:14.10) online. They are dated "Bolzano Novembre 1935-XIV" and were adopted by the podestà, the appointed mayor, on 7 December 1935. No sheet read for this research bears Piacentini's name; they are signed by the Comune's engineer and the podestà. One sheet sets out the zones of the new city and states its size: "abitanti 100.000".

Another deals with the old town, and it is the most telling drawing in this library. Its legend has five colours: monuments, new buildings, demolitions, public gardens, private gardens. Yellow, for demolition, runs along the whole north side of Via dei Portici, the medieval arcaded street, round the grain market and on towards the tanners' street. New fronts with arcades are drawn in their place. The plan did not leave the old centre intact. It meant to rebuild the old arcades to the measure of the new ones.

## 1936–1938: objections

The heritage office for the region rejected part of the plan in 1936. The ministries in Rome were under pressure of another kind. "Vanno considerate le ragioni d'ordine politico", the ministry of education wrote to the prefect in April 1937: the reasons of a political order must be considered. And the ministry of public works that June: "Il Piano di Bolzano è stato oggetto della particolare attenzione di S.E. il Capo del Governo." The plan of Bolzano has had the particular attention of the head of government.

In 1937 the plan hung in Rome at the first national exhibition of town plans. A reviewer [praised](row:14.13) its reasoned layout and the square of the monument, and went on: "mentre non persuadono le eccessive, o per meglio dire totali demolizioni previste per le Vie dei Portici, del Museo, dei Conciapelle", whereas the excessive, or better the total, demolitions foreseen for the streets of the arcades, the museum and the tanners do not persuade.

The state's council of public works approved the plan that November, with conditions. Its stamp is on the sheets. A second set of sheets, [of July 1938](row:14.14), shows what the objections achieved: far less is touched in the old town. The same sheets push the new avenue on to a "Piazza Tiberio" by the abbey church of Gries and bring in the roads from Merano by a "Viale Cesare Augusto" and a "Viale Giulio Cesare".

## 1941: the law

[The law](row:14.17) approving the plan is dated 23 January 1941. Two of its articles show how the plan was meant to pay for itself. Compensation for expropriated land left out any rise in value caused by the plan. And owners of new buildings that had to have arcades were to leave them open as public way, "come pubbliche vie", without payment. The arcade of the new city was a legal duty before it was a style.

The following year the British War Office [printed a town plan](row:14.19) of Bolzano for its own purposes, "copied from an Italian plan dated 1936". It shows the square of the monument and the army corps building in bird's-eye view. A drawing made for the regime's visitors was now serving an enemy's staff.

# Taken away, moved, disguised

## The station, 1928

The regime changed the old town by taking away as much as by adding. The first thing a visitor saw was changed first. The Austrian station of 1865, which [a postcard](row:14.03) shows as it was, was rebuilt by the State Railways' architect Angiolo Mazzoni and reopened on 24 May 1928.

## A fountain, 1933

On the riverside promenade stood the Laurin fountain of 1907, named after the dwarf-king of the local saga and put up when the town advertised itself as the "Laurin-Stadt". Its inscription wished "Der Bürger Wohl zu mehren, dem fremden Gast zu Ehren", to increase the citizens' welfare and honour the foreign guest. [A postcard](row:14.07) shows it about 1930. In the night of 4 to 5 June 1933 Fascists wrecked it. The pieces went to the city museum and then to the war museum at Rovereto. The account rests on an encyclopaedia article; no order or court record of 1933 was found.

## A poet, 1935

Ettore Tolomei, the senator whose programme of 1923 listed the measures for making the province Italian, had asked for one thing about monuments: that the statue of Walther come off the main square. He wanted the Roman general Drusus in its place. Mussolini refused in the 1920s, because of what it would cost abroad, and only the square was renamed. In 1935 the statue was moved to a less central park.

The Touring Club's monthly [reported it](row:14.09) that July as an exchange already made: Bolzano had carried out "la rimozione della statua di Walther von der Vogelweider dalla piazza Vittorio Emanuele III, per sostituirvi quella di Druso, il fondatore della città", the removal of the statue of Walther to put in its place that of Drusus, the founder of the city. No Drusus was ever set up. A national magazine announced a replacement that did not happen.

## A museum, 1936

The city museum, opened in 1905, had a tall neo-Gothic tower with battlements; [a postcard of 1904](row:14.02) shows it new. Tolomei had called the museum a stronghold of Germandom. From 1936 the outside was remade to match the new city: the tower and the battlements came off and a lower flat roof went on. The museum reopened on 31 October 1937 under a new name. Its director, in [his guide of 1942](row:14.18), writes: "L'istituto assume in quel tempo la denominazione di Museo dell'Alto Adige." Before the door stood a Roman milestone, set up in the year 46 by the emperor Claudius. He does not mention the tower.

Nearby the savings bank's building of 1904 was flattened to the look of the new city in 1938. An architect writing in 1985 printed [photographs of both](row:14.21) as they had been, and called what was done to them a "cura fascista", a Fascist cure.

## Names

The same was done with names, in the opposite direction. The guide of 1942 calls the bridge over the Talvera "Ponte Claudio". The plan of 1938 has its Piazza Tiberio and its avenues of the two Caesars. In the old town what marked the place as German was moved, cut down or broken. Beside it what could be read as Roman was added.

## What came back

After the war the reversals were nearly all on one side. The statue of Walther returned to its square in 1981, after a citizens' campaign that began in 1976. The museum's tower was rebuilt in 1990. The Laurin fountain came back from Rovereto in 1993 and has stood on a square in the centre since 1996. The joining of Gries to Bolzano was never questioned.

# Who else was building

## Local offices

While the state built its city, local architects went on working, and some of them built in a modern manner of their own. The Bolzano office of Amonn & Fingerle, active from 1921 to 1940, [built](https://www.uibk.ac.at/de/archiv-baukunstgeschichte/sammlung/unsere-bestande/ammon-fingerle/) a department store with a cinema in 1928 and a "Zentralgarage" in 1929 that came close to the rationalism the regime's architects were then claiming. A journal of 1985 [says](row:8.10) of Marius Amonn that he "hat seit Beginn der faschistischen Ära gegen die Zerstörung des alten Stadtbildes gekämpft", fought from the beginning of the Fascist era against the destruction of the old townscape, and for a long time worked on solutions for the old town "die mit den faschistischen Ideen in Einklang zu bringen waren", that could be reconciled with Fascist ideas.

The first houses for state employees, of 1925, were designed by two Tyroleans who had just opened an office in the town: the architect Clemens Holzmeister and Luis Trenker, later famous as a film-maker. The open-air bath of 1932, one of the best modern buildings of those years, is by Ettore Sottsass, one of the winners of the plan competition, with a Bolzano architect.

So a flat roof in Bolzano does not by itself mean Rome, and the line between the regime's architecture and the town's is not clean. The same local architects also worked for the state.

## From where, and for whom

The designers of the main buildings came from Rome, Padua, Milan and Trentino; a few were local. The column that says more is the one for the clients. The monument was the state's. The station was the railways'. The blocks on the new square and in the old town belonged to the national insurance and pension institutes, the housing to the institute for state employees, the big building on the avenue to the army. The new city was paid for and owned from Rome, and its first residents were the state's own staff.

Where the builders and the stone came from is known for very few works. The building files in the city archive would name the contractors.

## Still open

- The orders for removing the Walther statue in 1935, for the work on the museum in 1936, and any record of the attack on the fountain in 1933.
- The pages of the architecture journals of 1929–30 and 1939 that published the competition and the plan. No open scan was found.
- Aerial photographs of 1945 and 1954, which the Province lists and does not yet serve.
- An open photograph of the new avenue or of the party's house under construction. The pictures that circulated nationally are in copyright; the open ones are tourists' views of the old town.
- Where the milestone of Claudius stands today.

# Libya, 1931–1940

In the 1930s the Fascist state built farming villages for Italian families outside Italy: about two dozen in Libya, a handful in Ethiopia, six on the islands of Rhodes and Kos. This research looked at them as designed things, to set them beside a workers' quarter in Bolzano. This text describes the villages themselves: who drew them, what a family was given, who was chosen to go, and what the places were called. It rests above all on one study, Vittoria Capresi's [thesis on the Libyan villages](row:9.07), which has a card for each of them.

## 1931–1937: the first villages

The model was tried by a tobacco company. From 1931 it settled 22 families from the Abruzzi at Tigrinna, in the Gebel Garian hills of Tripolitania, on plots of two hectares that would be theirs after thirty years. The church stood on a rise so that its tower could be seen from all round. Beside it were a school, the priest's house, a workers' club and a cooperative shop, all facing "una piazza chiaramente perimetrata da un muro", a square clearly bounded by a wall. The villages that followed kept to this.

In 1932 the state created an agency for Cyrenaica, the eastern half of the colony, and put it under the head of its office for internal migration, Luigi Razza. The agency divided the land, built the farmhouses and looked after the families. The colonial public-works office built the villages. In 1933–34 its engineer Mario Romano built four of them on the Gebel, the upland of Cyrenaica. One was called Beda Littoria. Another, first named Primavera, was renamed Razza after its patron died in 1935.

The first settlers came on trial. In the autumn of 1933, 150 heads of family arrived alone for three months; their families joined them early in 1934 if they were found "idonei", suitable. In these first villages the farmhouses stood in groups of four to six double houses, for safety, round a shared well.

From 1935 the national social-insurance institute settled families in Tripolitania, the western half, paying with its unemployment funds and taking them "dalle regioni dove la disoccupazione era più alta", from the regions where unemployment was highest. The farm census of 1937 counted 2,711 Italian farming families in all Libya.

## 1938: the Twenty Thousand

In May 1938 the governor, Italo Balbo, announced that twenty thousand settlers would sail together on 28 October, the anniversary of the March on Rome. In six months 10,000 Italian and 23,000 Libyan workers built 1,800 farms and their villages. A decree of that May gave the programme its name, "colonizzazione demografica intensiva", intensive demographic colonisation, and made the state the supplier of all the land and all the capital.

Nine steamers left Genoa on 29 October. Six more took the southerners on board at Naples the next day, after a mass on a pier named after Razza. They landed at Tripoli on 2 November, where, in [the newsreel](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000030534/2/l-arrivo-20-000-rurali-italiani-africa.html), the bishop blesses the settlers on their knees in the castle square and Balbo salutes at Mussolini's statue.

The official photograph book called them "fanterie rurali", rural infantry, and showed Balbo visiting the families on the ships. [A study of the book](row:9.09) points to another record of the same voyage, the diary of one of the settlers, a peasant from Friuli named Giacomo Cason. The censor passed 682 settlers' letters to Balbo that November. One, from a family of the province of Ferrara, reads: "la mia casa sembra una villa, la stalla è migliore della casa che abitavo prima". My house looks like a villa; the stable is better than the house I lived in before.

Mussolini was not pleased. His foreign minister noted on 7 November that the Duce "si è seccato per lo strombazzamento fatto da Balbo", was annoyed by the trumpeting Balbo had made. In future the settlers were to leave "in piccoli scaglioni", in small batches.

## The village

A Libyan "centro rurale" was not where the farmers lived. The decree of 1938 defines it as "un nucleo di edifici destinati ad assicurare servizi pubblici", a core of buildings to provide public services: the church with the priest's house, the schools with the teachers' lodgings, a clinic, "la Casa del fascio", the post office, the markets. Capresi suggests it would be truer to call it a logistics centre. The families lived out on their farms.

The public-works office under the engineer Silvio Camilletti was overloaded and hired architects by contract. Their names are on the perspective drawings: Florestano Di Fausto, who advised on every building in the colony and never moved his office from Rome; Umberto Di Segni, a Jew born in Tripoli, who drops out of the colony's yearbooks after 1938; Giovanni Pellegrini, trained in Milan and in practice in Tripoli; Alfredo Longarini from Perugia. Each village was laid out round a square and fixed in its form from the day it was built.

There was no standard party house. Its fixed parts were a tower, a balcony for speeches and the lictor's bundle. In a farming village it served "più come un dopolavoro", more as a workers' club. Where a village lay on the coast road the building faced the road, "così da fare pubblicità di sé", so as to advertise itself to those passing.

The architects made the places look familiar. At the village D'Annunzio the roofs are pitched, which Capresi reads as a "ponte semantico", a bridge of meaning, for peasants from the Abruzzi. The church of the village Crispi recalls Sant'Andrea in Mantua.

## The farm

After 1938 each farmhouse stood by itself on its land, "con lo specifico scopo di avvicinare anche psicologicamente il colono al proprio terreno", with the specific aim of bringing the settler closer to his land, psychologically too. At one village the governor ordered the houses set at the exact centre of each plot.

The house had one storey and three or four rooms, with a stable, a barn, an oven and a dung pit. A farm in Cyrenaica measured about 30 hectares, most of it plain arable with seven hectares of olives. Irrigated farms in Tripolitania had 10 to 15 hectares, dry ones about 50. A family in Cyrenaica was given two cows, two oxen, a horse, a pig, ten hens and a cock, and a plough, a harrow and a cart.

None of it was theirs yet. At the villages Crispi and Gioda a family worked two years for wages and three as sharecroppers, and then began paying off the cost of the farm, less a state grant of a third. It was expected to own the farm "in 19 anni". Elsewhere the term was about thirty years.

## Who went

The settlers were chosen as whole families: large, used to the land, able to read, members of the party. Balbo wrote that the colonisation of Libya "è quintessenza di fascismo e deve essere compiuta da fascisti di fede sicura", is the quintessence of Fascism and must be carried out by Fascists of sure faith. A Tripoli newspaper described the procedure in October 1938. The farm workers' union collected the applications for the party. A first choice went to the commissariat for internal migration in Rome. Each family was examined by a doctor and checked for its politics, its trade, its morals and its criminal record. Then a commission visited it at home.

They came above all from the Veneto and the Po delta. For 1939 the same newspaper printed a list by province: Padua 203 families, Treviso 193, Venice 166, Udine 102, then Vicenza, Verona and the south. On that list the provinces of the Veneto make up a little over half, and with Udine about 62 per cent. Seven families came from the province of Trento.

## The names

The villages for Italians were named after people, and the names fall into groups: martyrs of the Fascist squads, aviators, heroes of the Risorgimento, prophets of empire. Four bear the names of men executed by Austria as Italian irredentists: Battisti and Oberdan in 1938, Filzi and Sauro in 1939. The Fascist daily of Trento [told its readers](row:9.11) in October 1938 that families would go to villages named "Baracca, Oberdan, D'Annunzio e del trentino Cesare Battisti".

The villages built for Muslim Libyans had names of another kind: Alba, Fiorita, Deliziosa, Verde. A colonial review explained in 1939 that Italian villages carry "i nomi di eroi della nostra storia", the names of heroes of our history, and the Muslim ones names that suit "la poesia del mondo musulmano". One of them, Fiorita, had 22 farms of two hectares.

## 1939–1940: the second wave and the end

The settlers of 1939 left more quietly, from Venice, Naples and Palermo: 1,465 families and 10,907 people. The plan had been for 3,000 farms; 898 were laid out for the land agency and 540 for the insurance institute. In Venice the Patriarch [blessed them](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000023085/2/arrivano-venezia-i-dieci-treni-speciali-nuove-famiglie-coloni-venuti-imbarcarsi-libia.html) in Piazza San Marco. For 1940 the plan shrank to forty farms near a village that exists only as a project. Eleven houses were finished. No family came.

At the end of 1939 the land agency held 2,817 farms for 2,716 families. The historian Roberta Pergher [counts](row:10.20) "close to 30,000 Italians being settled" in Libya, against a target of half a million.

# Ethiopia, the Aegean and the way back to the Alps

## Rhodes and Kos, 1929–1939

The Aegean islands Italy had held since 1912 got their villages before Libya's great waves. On Rhodes a private company began one in 1929. Three more followed in 1935–38, and two on Kos. Each had, in one summary, "una chiesa, una scuola, una casa del fascio, uffici comunali, una caserma dei carabinieri, un ambulatorio medico e alcune botteghe", and some had a cinema and an inn.

One of them was made for people from the Alps. On 14 October 1934 the governor, Mario Lago, [wrote](row:9.11) to a forestry engineer from Trentino, Giuseppe Valcanover, proposing "uno o più villaggi di boscaioli delle nostre montagne", one or more villages of woodcutters from our mountains. Valcanover had worked for the state forests in Alto Adige from 1918 to 1932, and his family lived in Bolzano. The first seven heads of family, from the Fiemme valley, sailed in April 1935. German-speaking families from South Tyrol followed in 1936. The village, Campochiaro, stood inland at 300 metres: "nuovo, bianco, mediterraneo", new, white and Mediterranean, with houses ready on arrival. In 1938 it had some 280 people.

In 1939 most of the South Tyroleans left. They had opted for Germany. "Da Rodi noi dovemmo partire per le Opzioni", one of them, Ludwig Kompatscher of Nova Levante, said later. From Rhodes we had to leave for the Options.

One of the two architects of Campochiaro, Armando Bernabiti, had begun his career in 1924 at the public-housing institute of Rome.

## Ethiopia, 1936–1940

In Ethiopia the regime tried something different: to move pieces of Italian regions. Three decrees of 6 December 1937 created agencies called Romagna d'Etiopia, Puglia d'Etiopia and Veneto d'Etiopia. The Veneto agency meant to transfer "nuclei omogenei di coloni", homogeneous groups of settlers, 1,500 heads of family in six years, and to recreate for them "un ambiente simile a quello lasciato", a setting like the one they had left. It never started. A historian of the emigration from Trentino [remarks](row:9.11) that the choice of regions was no accident: Romagna was Mussolini's land and Puglia the party secretary's.

Life there was hard and military. The men were enrolled in a workers' legion of the Fascist militia for two years, on a daily wage, before any of them got a farm. The Romagna agency had fewer than a hundred settlers by 1940, none joined by his family. The Puglia agency's settlement, Bari d'Etiopia, had 104 settlers, families included. One of them wrote home in 1940: "pioveva dappertutto nella casa piena di umidità ... Siamo trattati come schiavi." It rained everywhere into the damp house; we are treated like slaves. [The study](row:9.08) that quotes him estimates that in all Ethiopia there were no more than 400 real farmer-settlers.

The veterans' organisation that had drained the marshes south of Rome ran two estates near Addis Ababa, on land taken from the emperor. By January 1939 it had nearly finished 92 houses of 60 square metres, built "per avere un aspetto che risultasse familiare ai coloni", to look familiar to the settlers. The viceroy complained that they were too small. Of 90 families proposed, the migration commissariat found 56 fit. The first group, which left Brindisi on 20 February 1939, included eight families "della Venezia Tridentina", from the region of Trento and Bolzano.

## Back to the Alps

The overseas villages and South Tyrol touch at several points, and this research set out to find them.

They touch in people. Razza had been a Fascist organiser in Trento before he ran the migration office and the Cyrenaican agency. Valcanover went from the forests of Alto Adige to Rhodes.

They touch in families. Five families from Trentino, 32 people, left for Cyrenaica in October 1938, and more in 1939. South Tyroleans went to Rhodes and came back for the Option.

They touch on film. The same art director of Luce, Arturo Gemmiti, signed the newsreels of the Twenty Thousand and, in October 1939, the newsreel of Bolzano's new workers' quarter.

And they touch in words. At the founding of a new town south of Rome in April 1938 Mussolini [began](https://patrimonio.archivioluce.com/luce-web/detail/IL5000028366/2/25-aprile-1938-fondazione.html): "Italiani, dalle Alpi alla Libia oggi è un giorno di festa", Italians, from the Alps to Libya today is a day of celebration. The same year Ettore Tolomei, the senator who had written the programme for making South Tyrol Italian, praised the "splendid exodus to Libya of twenty thousand settlers" and asked that in Alto Adige too the state place "at least a few dozen families in every municipality". The words are known here only in Pergher's English.

What was not found is a drawing. No architect of the Libyan villages is known to have worked in Bolzano, and no colonial farmhouse plan to have reached its housing institute. The likeness between a farm in Cyrenaica and a workers' house with a garden in Bolzano, a house, a plot and a family feeding itself, seems to come from a doctrine both shared.

## Still open

- Plans and architects for the settlements in Ethiopia and for four of the six Aegean villages.
- How the Libyan farmhouses were built: dimensions, walls, roofs. The study used says almost nothing about construction.
- The full list by province of the families of 1938. The partial one names Ferrara, Verona and the Polesine first.
- Whether the Bolzano papers reported the Twenty Thousand, and with which names.
- Tolomei's sentence of 1938 in Italian.

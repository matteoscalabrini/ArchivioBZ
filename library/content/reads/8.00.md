# The house and the plot, 1937–1943

The Semirurali were a quarter of small houses with gardens that the Fascist housing institute built for factory workers on the south bank of the Isarco in Bolzano. Who built it, under which laws and with what relation to the regime's colonies is told in [another text of this library](row:0.02). This one stays close to the object: the house, the plot, the people in it, and what was done with the quarter after the war.

## 1937: against the town plan

The housing institute of Bolzano had begun in 1935 with tall blocks in via Torino. Tenants complained about the rents. At the same time, by the account of the architect who wrote the quarter's history, the regime sent out "nuove direttive", new directives, against the growth of cities. In 1937 the institute's technical director, the engineer Giulio Garbini, "chiamato da Venezia", called from Venice, [drew a first overall proposal](row:8.09).

It ran against the town plan, which foresaw only dense public housing here. Garbini used a clause that allowed grouped small houses and laid out some thirty blocks on about 70 hectares, with four kinds of street: "il viale alberato, la strada di distribuzione, la traversa, il percorso pedonale", the tree-lined avenue, the distributor road, the cross street, the footpath. The centre was a square called piazza Pontinia. The streets were named after Italian cities, and the names can be read on his plan: Milano, Udine, Belluno, Brescia, Palermo, Aosta, Cagliari, Sassari, Bari, Mantova, Vercelli.

## The types

Garbini [worked out](row:4.08) "tre tipi edilizi a due stanze e tre tipi a tre stanze", three types with two rooms and three with three. The models in the quarter's museum show them. Four of the types are houses of four dwellings on two floors, and they differ in where the stair to the upper flats is put: on the front, at the back, at the side. A fifth has an inside stair and was "destinato a famiglie numerose", meant for large families, with 74 square metres. A sixth, of 1940, has four shops below four flats; two such houses made the eight shops of the square.

A flat had two or three rooms on 40 to 60 square metres. The kitchen was a niche off the living room. There was one sink and a closet with a toilet, and [no bathroom, cellar, attic or storeroom](row:4.10). The houses were built to the standard of the "casa minima", the minimum dwelling, of cheap materials from within the country, "senza un adeguato isolamento da freddo e umidità", [without adequate protection](row:4.20) against cold and damp. On the drawings the type that survives in via Bari is 16.30 metres wide.

The outside stair was given a meaning. When the Duchess of Pistoia visited in December 1938, the regime's German-language daily [wrote](row:8.04): "Jedes Häuschen hat seinen Garten und mit dem freien Aufgang erinnern die Bauten an das Geburtshaus des Duce." Every little house has its garden, and with their open stair the buildings recall the house where the Duce was born.

## The garden

Each house of four dwellings stood on 600 square metres. That leaves about 150 for each family, the house's footprint included.

The plot came bare. In August 1939 the same paper [reported](row:8.05) a competition for the best gardens and described what the residents had been given: "ein kleines Stück Grund", a small piece of ground, that "nichts weiter war als ein Stück unbebautes Land", was nothing more than a piece of untilled land. The Dopolavoro, the regime's leisure organisation, handed out seed. The institute saw to the watering and distributed a booklet on how to keep a garden. Garbini sat on the jury.

People built on it. "Tutti del quartiere nell'orto avevano costruito una baracca, un piccolo pollaio dove quasi tutti avevano delle galline", a former resident [told the museum](row:4.20): everyone in the quarter had built a shed in the garden, a small coop, and nearly everyone had hens. Photographs of 1939 show a rabbit hutch.

In the war the gardens became policy. In May 1941 the party's provincial secretary [handed out vegetable seed](https://claudiaugusta-bolzano-scomparsa.provincia.bz.it/1941.html) to the residents in the presence of 360 "massaie rurali", rural housewives. In April 1942 the institute [assigned](https://claudiaugusta-bolzano-scomparsa.provincia.bz.it/1942.html) 105 war gardens of 100 square metres each.

The regime's magazine in Bolzano praised gardens "ove gettare un seme e raccogliere un frutto cresciuto con la propria cura quotidiana", where to sow a seed and gather a fruit grown by one's own daily care, "per non dimenticare del tutto le origini rurali", so as not to forget one's rural origins altogether. The city's own exhibition booklet [reads the garden](row:4.20) more coldly: a plot to make up for low wages, and an attempt to keep a working class tied "alle occupazioni e ai valori della terra", to the occupations and values of the land.

## Who got one

No rule for the allocation of the flats has been found. What the sources show is an order of events: first the job, then the flat. "Ha trovato lavoro alla Lancia e in poco tempo ha ricevuto anche l'appartamento", one resident said of his father. Another: "L'Istituto Fascista delle Case Popolari ci assegnò questa casa in via Vercelli." The institute assigned us this house in via Vercelli.

Large families were preferred, or at least shown. At the inauguration on 8 October 1939 the party secretary was taken to see "la famiglia dei coniugi Turati … di ben dodici figli", [the Turati couple's family](row:8.06) of no fewer than twelve children.

The tenants came, in the booklet's words, "in larga parte dalle campagne venete", largely from the Veneto countryside. The housing institute's own retrospect of the 1980s [says](row:8.11) the garden city was meant to solve "il problema psicologico dell'inurbamento di popolazioni rurali", the psychological problem of bringing rural people into a town.

## What it cost, what it was called

The 808 dwellings counted at the inauguration [cost](row:3.26) 22 million lire, about 27,000 lire each. Rents are known only for the same institute's houses in Bressanone: about 125 lire a month for three rooms, 95 for two.

The Italian daily called the place "il rione Dux delle case semi-rurali", "rione nuovo di zecca, sorto in pochi mesi come per incanto nelle campagne di S. Quirino": a brand-new quarter, risen in a few months as if by magic in the fields of San Quirino. The regime's magazine called it "pittoresco e civettuolo", picturesque and coquettish, its houses standing "fra la casa poderale cittadina e la casa tipo poderale delle campagne", between the town house and the farmhouse.

The people of the older workers' quarter next door called it Shanghai.

By the time building stopped in 1942–43 the quarter had its services: a nursery, a mother-and-child clinic, public baths in via Rovigo with seven tubs and nine showers, the seat of the party's neighbourhood group, a school of eight classrooms that people called "le Scuolette". A trolleybus from the station was put off until after the war.

# Replaced, 1957–2015

## 1957: public housing as an accusation

In 1957 the quarter held about 5,000 people in 1,100 dwellings. Its end began that year with a protest that was not about it.

The state planned a large new quarter of public housing in Bolzano, and a South Tyrolean rally at Castelfirmiano protested against it. The central committee of the South Tyrolean People's Party [wrote](row:8.09): "Roma, si affretta a compiere atti tendenti a modificare il carattere popolare del nostro paese. Come mezzo principale per raggiungere questo scopo, l'Italia si serve dell'edilizia popolare." Rome hastens to carry out acts that tend to change the character of our people; as the principal means to this end, Italy uses public housing. The charge was that the Republic went on building the city with the instruments of Fascism.

Out of that conflict came a compromise. New public housing would not take more farmland. It would go where the institute already owned the ground, on the plots of the Semirurali, and it would go up.

## 1959–1976: house by house

In 1959 "cinque case Semirurali all'angolo di via Milano e via Palermo vengono demolite e sostituite da 60 alloggi in edifici a 5 piani": five houses at the corner of via Milano and via Palermo were demolished and replaced by 60 flats in five-storey buildings. The town plan of 1964 foresaw the replacement of all of them. Block followed block: 112 flats in 1964–66, 200 in via Aosta in 1969–72, 106 in via Brescia in 1975–76. By 1976 there were 478 new flats where low houses had stood.

## 1976–1990: the plan

In 1976 the institute commissioned a plan for the whole area, and the Province approved it in January 1978. Its figures [are in the institute's own account](row:8.11): "220.000 m2 di terreno, triplicazione della densità edilizia, costruzione di ca. 2.000 alloggi in sostituzione degli attuali 800". Twenty-two hectares, three times the density, some 2,000 flats in place of the 800 still standing. The first design meant seven enormous courts a hundred metres square, seven storeys high.

Well-known architects built the lots. Carlo Aymonino was commissioned for the first [in October 1978](row:8.10). The English practice Darbourne & Darke won the competition for the second in 1981. In December 1983 the density was cut from 5 to 3.5 cubic metres per square metre of ground. The residents gave the new buildings names of their own: Aymonino's block was the "Alcazar", the English architects' houses were the "case inglesi", the third lot was the "muro", the wall.

The 1930s had used the picture of a village to settle Italians in Bolzano. The decades after 1957 used the same land for the same people in the opposite form. That reading is this research's own.

## 1986–2015: one house

In March 1986 the diggers on the site hit the remains of a medieval church, Santa Maria in Augia. The find was protected, and the housing institute kept a few of the old houses standing near it, one of them at via Bari 11, on the corner of via Alessandria.

The association La Fabbrica del Tempo proposed a museum in 1995. A working group met from 1999 to 2004 and published a book. The furniture of the Mentzel family, who had lived at via Palermo 124 from 1939 to 1984, was bought for it. A park around the church remains opened in 2007. The Comune acquired the house in 2010, and on 16 May 2015 it opened to visitors, with one ground-floor flat kept nearly as its last tenants left it and the recorded voices of former residents.

One of the voices is German. "1949, im Oktober, sind wir von Graz als Rückoptanten nach Bozen gekommen": in October 1949 we came from Graz to Bolzano as returning optants, people who had left for the Reich in the Option and come back. In 1981, by a figure in the architects' journal, the quarter had 1,112 German speakers, twice as many as ten years before.

Another voice sums up the Italian side. "Siamo diventati tutti bolzanini … anche il nostro dialetto non era un dialetto veneto o un dialetto calabrese ma un dialetto bolzanino, era un misto." We all became people of Bolzano; even our dialect was not a Veneto dialect or a Calabrian one but a Bolzano dialect, it was a mixture.

Not everyone who has written about the quarter trusts that warmth. The historian Andrea Di Michele [has criticised](row:4.10) the nostalgic memoirs and described the Semirurali as an instrument of "segregazione e di indottrinamento", segregation and indoctrination, of a suburb kept apart.

# The crypt, and a poster of 1929

Under the Monument to Victory in Bolzano there is an exhibition, *BZ '18–'45: one monument, one city, two dictatorships*. Among its exhibits hang five printed things in recessed steel frames: three posters, the cover of a pamphlet and the cover of a magazine. The labels say little about them. On the morning of 14 November 2026 they were photographed for this library, and each was then traced: what it is, who made it, where an original is kept, and what happened on the occasion it was printed for. This text tells what was found, in the order the five were made, from 1929 to 1938.

## How the pictures were made

The exhibition shows the five as reproductions, printed on board and set back in their boxes. They hang above eye level, so each was photographed from below and a little from the side. The pictures here are those photographs cut to the sheet and pulled square by a small program written for this library.

The proportions were not assumed. A rectangle seen at an angle gives away its own shape once the lens is known, and the five sheets came out with their corners at 90.0 to 90.3 degrees. Nothing was retouched. The shadow of a frame and a patch of sun are still on the paper.

Forty-nine photographs were taken that morning, and all but a duplicate and a blurred one are in the library: the five pieces, [the two eagle heads](row:17.25) from the Druso bridge that sit in the crypt, the frescoes, the exhibition's own panels, and the monument and the column behind it from outside.

## 1929: a winged Victory with the fasces in her arm

The earliest of the five is a poster on a red ground. A dark winged Victory in a long robe strides down a flight of steps towards the viewer. She lifts a laurel spray in her right hand and carries a large fasces with its axe under her left arm. Behind her is a pale triumphal arch. A black band gives the occasion: "Iª ESPOSIZIONE NAZIONALE dell'ALTO ADIGE / 10 AGOSTO – BOLZANO – 10 SETTEMBRE / sotto l'Alto Patronato del Capo del Governo / BENITO MUSSOLINI".

![A poster: a winged figure with raised arm before a white monument, lettering below.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/images/1929_Esposizione_Nazionale_Alto_Adige_poster_visit.jpg)
The poster of the first national exhibition of the Alto Adige, 1929, as reproduced in the exhibition under the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026, cut to the sheet and set square. [Row 17.01](row:17.01).

An original is in the national poster collection at Treviso. [Its record](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0500662517) describes the subject as "vittoria alata saluta con fascio littorio in mano davanti al Monumento alla Vittoria di Bolzano", a winged Victory saluting with the lictor's bundle in her hand before the Monument to Victory. The sheet measures 100 by 70 centimetres and was printed by Carlo Perego in Milan. The record names no artist. The Treviso sheet has a line that the copy in the crypt lacks, "RIDUZIONI FERROVIARIE DEL 50 %", and the crypt's copy is taller in shape: it reproduces another edition of the same drawing. A signature on it has not been read.

The exhibition was real and large. Mussolini authorised it by decree on 27 March 1929 and took its honorary presidency. On the opening day the Fascist daily of Bolzano [headed its front page](row:17.02) "La sagra del lavoro a Bolzano", the festival of labour, and began: "Nessuna barriera divide ormai più l'Alto Adige dal resto della Nazione." No barrier any longer divides the Alto Adige from the rest of the nation. For want of one building large enough, the show was spread over eight, most of them schools. The mayor's proclamation, in the regime's German-language paper, called Bolzano "die zwei Mal erlöste Stadt", the twice redeemed city, and "der wachsame Posten Italiens an seinen unverletzlichen Grenzen", Italy's watchful post on its inviolable borders.

At the close, on 11 September, the same paper [reported](row:17.03) more than 100,000 visitors, 28 public bodies "aus den alten Provinzen", from the old provinces, and over 800 firms. The minister of transport had granted half fare on the railways for the whole month. Mussolini ordered that such an exhibition be held in Bolzano every third year. A second was being prepared in 1931. It does not seem to have taken place.

The drawing does not copy the real monument closely. Piacentini's building has columns in the form of fasces; the poster shows an arch with fluted pilasters, and gives the fasces to the figure. On this research's reading the bundle has moved from the architecture into the Victory's arms, and she is leaving the monument for the town.

# 1932: a pamphlet from Munich

## Two spiders

The second piece is the cover of a booklet. On a pale ground lies a map of Tyrol in three pink pieces, with the names Kufstein, Innsbruck, Lienz, Sterzing, Meran and Bozen. Over it is spun one web, whose hub sits near Sterzing, just south of the Brenner. Two spiders hang in it. The upper one, with a white swastika on its black back, reaches towards a dot marked München. The lower one hangs towards a dot marked Rom. The title is lettered by hand: "Deutsch-Südtirol in Schmerz und Schand!", German South Tyrol in pain and shame, and at the lower right a question: "Mit ihm das ganze deutsche Land?" With it the whole German land?

![A booklet cover: a pink map under a spider's web with two spiders.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/images/1932_Steinberger_Deutsch-Suedtirol_cover_visit.jpg)
Ludwig Steinberger, *Deutsch-Südtirol in Schmerz und Schand!*, Munich, July 1932: the cover as reproduced in the exhibition under the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026, cut to the sheet and set square. [Row 17.06](row:17.06).

It is a pamphlet of 53 pages by Ludwig Steinberger, a historian at the University of Munich, published in July 1932 by a press that called itself "Der Antifaschist". It cost 60 pfennigs. On 24 July, a week before the German parliamentary election, a Catholic weekly in Munich that fought the Nazis [advertised it](row:17.05) as new: "Mit umfangreichem Tatsachenmaterial weist der Historiker den Verrat Hitlers an Südtirol nach." With extensive factual material the historian proves Hitler's betrayal of South Tyrol.

Catalogues date it wrongly to 1933. The advertisement, a note of the Bavarian State Library and [a review](row:17.07) of January 1933 that says it had appeared "vor einiger Zeit", some time ago, all put it in the summer of 1932.

## The author

Steinberger was born in Munich in 1879 and died there in 1968. He had written on the Brenner and on Tyrolean place names since 1911, and from 1926 held a professorship without a chair. The Bavarian State Library's catalogue carries a note on the pamphlet: because of it, "wurde der Verfasser am 27.06.1933 als Professor aus dem Staatsdienst entlassen", the author was dismissed from the civil service as a professor on 27 June 1933, since it attacked Hitler's policy on South Tyrol. He went into exile in Tyrol, fled to Switzerland when Austria was annexed, was stripped of his German citizenship in October 1941, and came back in 1946.

## The text

The booklet itself is online, in the provincial library at Bolzano, which holds a copy that the big catalogues do not list. It was [read there on screen](row:17.06) for this research on the day of the visit: the foreword and the second half in full, about ten pages of the first half not at all.

The foreword is by another hand and is signed "Andreas Andernach". It states the argument of the cover in words. Whoever helps the Fascist dictatorship into the saddle in Germany should learn what Fascism is, "ob er nun in schwarzen oder braunen Hosen marschiert", whether it marches in black trousers or brown.

Steinberger's own chapters first claim Tyrol on both sides of the Brenner as German land, and then record Italian rule since 1919 under the heading "Deutsch-Südtirols Kreuzweg", South Tyrol's way of the cross. Bolzano is in it by name. He describes the attack on the trade-fair procession of 24 April 1921, in which a teacher, Franz Innerhofer, was shot while sheltering two children in a doorway. At the funeral, he writes, thousands sang the Andreas Hofer song with its line "Ganz Deutschland, ach, in Schmach und Schmerz", all Germany in shame and pain, "mit ihm das Land Tirol", and with it the land of Tyrol. The cover turns the line round. In the song Tyrol shares Germany's shame. In 1932 Germany is asked whether it wants South Tyrol's.

He is exact about how the rule was felt: in names and papers. The regime's German-language daily, the *Alpenzeitung*, he calls a "journalistische Giftpflanze erster Ordnung", a journalistic poison plant of the first order, meant to bring foreign poison to the people in a German vessel. He quotes a prefect: "Das Alto Adige muß mit einem italienischen Firnis überzogen werden." The Alto Adige must be coated with an Italian varnish. And he answers that the varnish flakes: under a pasted-on place name like Colle Isarco or Brunico, a Gossensaß and a Bruneck show through.

Half the booklet is a file of dated quotations against Hitler. The central one is from a meeting in Munich on 14 November 1922: Germany must go together with Italy, and for that "ist nötig ein klarer und bündiger Verzicht auf die Deutschen in Südtirol", a clear and binding renunciation of the Germans in South Tyrol is needed. "In der Politik gibt es keine Sentiments, sondern nur Kaltschnäuzigkeit." In politics there are no sentiments, only cold-bloodedness. Steinberger limits his own charge with care. Hitler had no hand in giving South Tyrol to Italy. But a German who stabs in the back those struggling for their German nationhood "muß sich den Vorwurf des Südtirolverrates im weiteren Betrachte … gefallen lassen", must put up with the charge of betraying South Tyrol in the wider sense.

Two of his reports concern the monument under which his cover now hangs. On 9 December 1931, he writes, three National Socialists from Germany laid a wreath there "in Anwesenheit fascistischer Milizleute", in the presence of Fascist militiamen. And on 21 January 1932 a Bolzano group of the Nazi party was founded in an inn in the Bindergasse. Both rest on this one polemical source; the newspapers he cites have not been opened.

## Why it hangs there

In the exhibition the cover hangs at a station called "The two fascisms shake hands", above [a photograph](row:5.06) of 28 October 1932, when a delegation of German Nazis came to the monument for the tenth anniversary of the March on Rome. The pamphlet had come out three months earlier. The exhibition uses it as a picture of its time. What it says inside is not on the wall.

# 1933–1938: a week, a cover, a column

## 1933: a poster by competition

The third piece is another exhibition poster, and this one is modern. On a dark ground stand a white block with three slots, a dark upright bound with two straps in the national colours, a bunch of grapes, a hammer and pincers, and a fox. The lettering is Italian only: "SETTIMANA DELL'ALTO ADIGE / RIDUZIONI FERROVIARIE DEL 70% / BOLZANO 22–31 OTTOBRE XI". It is signed Ant. Hofer.

![A poster in dark tones: a white block, a bound upright, grapes, tools and a fox.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/images/1933_Hofer_Settimana_Alto_Adige_poster_visit.jpg)
Anton Hofer, *Settimana dell'Alto Adige*, October 1933, as reproduced in the exhibition under the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026, cut to the sheet and set square. [Row 17.10](row:17.10).

Anton Hofer was born in Bozen in 1888, trained in Vienna under Koloman Moser, worked with the Wiener Werkstätte and came home after the war. The Treviso collection [holds an original](https://sigecweb.beniculturali.it/sigec/item/print/ICCD11161972) of his poster and reads the white block as the Monument to Victory and the upright as the fasces.

Every motif on it was ordered. On 29 August 1933 the *Alpenzeitung* [printed the rules](row:17.08) of a competition open to all artists of the province. The poster had to measure 1 metre by 0.70, to be printed in three colours on a ground, and to show one of the week's events, the agricultural show, the crafts show or the fur-animal show, with an allegorical reference to the times. Nineteen designs came in. [The jury](row:17.09), chaired by the mayor, chose Hofer's. Franz Lenhart, who drew most of the province's tourist posters, received an honourable mention.

So the grapes are the farm show, the tools the crafts, the fox the furs, and the block and the bundle the reference to the times. Five years after its inauguration the monument had become a sign that could stand in a trade poster beside a fox.

The week opened on 22 October 1933 with a wreath at the monument. On 29 October the *Alpenzeitung* [reported](row:17.11) that all tickets were sold out three days before the end and that the show would stay open until 11 November, with the 70 per cent fare extended for visitors from outside.

## 1935: the Duce on the steps

The fourth piece is the cover of the weekly *L'Illustrazione Italiana* for 1 September 1935. A photograph fills it: Mussolini climbing the steps of the monument between two officers. The caption reads: "Le giornate del Duce nell'entusiasmo di Bolzano italiana e fascista. Mussolini ascende la scalea del monumento che rammemora la vittoria di ieri ed auspica quella di domani." The Duce's days amid the enthusiasm of Italian and Fascist Bolzano; Mussolini climbs the steps of the monument that recalls yesterday's victory and augurs tomorrow's.

![A magazine cover with a photograph of men in uniform climbing wide steps.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/images/1935-09-01_Illustrazione_Italiana_cover_visit.jpg)
*L'Illustrazione Italiana*, 1 September 1935, cover, as reproduced in the exhibition under the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026, cut to the sheet and set square. [Row 17.14](row:17.14).

Tomorrow's victory was Ethiopia, which Italy invaded on 3 October.

The local papers give the day. Mussolini arrived by special train shortly after noon on Monday 26 August 1935, on his first visit to the city, for the army's manoeuvres. In the afternoon he went round the new Italian Bolzano: the industrial zone, the building site of the workers' houses, the new army command, the monument, the party's house. The old town was not on the list. At the monument, the *Alpenzeitung* [wrote](row:17.12), he laid a laurel wreath, stood for a minute at attention and gave the Fascist salute. *Dolomiten* [adds](row:17.13) that the prefect that day "trug die Uniform eines Alpini-Majors", wore the uniform of a major of the Alpine troops. That fits the officer at the left of the picture, and the party secretary would be the man at the right. The cover names neither, and the identification is this research's.

## 1938: a column from Rome

The last piece is a poster. It shows the monument with a slender column standing before it, and reads: "INAUGURAZIONE DELLA / COLONNA ROMANA / DEDICATA AI CADUTI DELL'ALTO ADIGE / IN TERRA D'AFRICA E IN ISPAGNA / BOLZANO 5 giugno A·XVI". Inauguration of the Roman column dedicated to the fallen of the Alto Adige in Africa and in Spain. At the margin are two letters, "FR.", which nobody has yet attached to a name.

![A poster: a column in front of a monument, with lettering above.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/images/1938_Colonna_Romana_inauguration_poster_visit.jpg)
*Inaugurazione della Colonna Romana*, poster, Bolzano, 5 June 1938, as reproduced in the exhibition under the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026, cut to the sheet and set square. [Row 17.16](row:17.16).

The column was a gift of the city of Rome. It was unveiled on Whit Sunday, 5 June 1938, in the park behind the monument, before the crown prince and princess. The archbishop of Trento blessed it. The Fascist daily [describes](row:17.18) how the father of a decorated airman killed in Spain pulled away the tricolour cloth, and gives the inscription: "Agli alto atesini — Caduti per l'Impero — Roma riconoscente — A. XVI". To the people of the Alto Adige fallen for the Empire, a grateful Rome. The families of thirty-five dead stood by.

Two weeks earlier the party secretary had read out the names at the monument, each answered by drums and the cry "Presente". *Dolomiten* [printed them](row:17.15). There are thirty-two on its page, many of them German. One, killed near Lérida in March 1938, was called Andreas Hofer, like the Tyrolean hero of 1809. The exhibition's own panel draws the point: since many of the fallen belonged to the German population, the regime could claim them for its wars.

The poster's view cannot be found on the ground. It puts the column in front of the monument's east face; the column stands in the park on the other side. It gives the column a capital and steps that it does not have. It is an emblem.

![A plain stone column on a square base in a park.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/17_monument_visit/photos/IMG_1795.JPG)
The Roman column of 1938 in the park behind the monument. Own photograph, 14 November 2026. [Row 17.19](row:17.19).

The column is still there. Its base reads "AGLI ATESINI / CADVTI PER / L IMPERO / ROMA / RICONOSCENTE / ANNO XVI E F". An information post of the exhibition stands a few metres off.

## The rest of the morning

Other things photographed that day closed open questions elsewhere in this library.

The two eagle heads from the Druso bridge are on show, with a label naming their sculptor. An earlier text here had not been able to say whether they still were.

A page of a Bolzano paper of [10 February 1926](row:17.21) prints, in a box, the sentence in which Mussolini announced the monument: it would rise "sulle stesse fondamenta sulle quali doveva sorgere il monumento della vittoria tedesca", on the same foundations on which the monument of the German victory was to rise. And a page of the *Innsbrucker Nachrichten* of [12 July 1928](row:17.24), the day of the inauguration, shows the view from the other side of the border. Under a drawing it writes of "das „Sieges“-Denkmal", the "Victory" monument, with the word victory in quotation marks.

The exhibition answers the monument in the monument's own medium. Against Latin capitals in marble it sets single words in plain capitals on banners: VIOLENCE, IDEOLOGY, VOTE. On a yellow arch it puts the word MONUMENT with a question mark.

## Still open

- Who drew the poster of 1929, and what the signature on the crypt's edition says.
- Who lent the five originals to the exhibition. Its panels name no lender.
- The pages of Steinberger's pamphlet not yet read, and the newspapers behind his report of a Nazi wreath at the monument in December 1931.
- Who ran the press "Der Antifaschist", and who drew its cover.
- Who "FR." was.
- The photographer of the magazine cover of 1935.
- Whether the national exhibition planned for 1932 was refused or dropped.

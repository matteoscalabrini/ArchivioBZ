# Letters, words and pages

Can a charged text or image be disarmed without being removed, by changing only its form? The typeface, one word, the colour, the size, the page, the voice that reads it? This section of the library collects thirty-seven attempts by artists, activists, publishers and programmers, and five of them are here because they show the idea failing.

The list began as a conversation with an AI assistant, which started from one narrow question: whether anyone had replaced the typefaces of Fascist propaganda with funny ones. Every item it named was then looked up at a source, and each has a row in the library that says what the source confirmed. This text gives the works as checked, grouped by what was changed and, within each group, in the order they were made.

Behind most of them stands a text of 1956. Guy Debord and Gil Wolman's [manual of *détournement*](row:12.01), the reuse of existing material against its first meaning, gives two rules that keep proving true below. Simple reversal "is always the most direct and the least effective". And the effect depends on "the conscious or semiconscious recollection of the original contexts": the audience has to remember what is being turned.

## The typeface

The nearest thing to an experiment is a quiz. In 2012 the film-maker Errol Morris [had readers](row:12.15) of the *New York Times* judge a passage that appeared, at random, in one of six typefaces. About 45,000 answered. "Comic Sans has the lowest rate of agreement." The effect was small, and the result people remember is that Baskerville did best.

A font can also be made to refuse words. Roel Nieskens's [Sans Bullshit Sans](row:12.20) of 2015 replaces every buzzword on its list with a censor's bar, using nothing but the feature that normally joins an f to an i.

In 2023 a design student in London, Rada Musikarat, [collected](row:12.31) Nazi newspapers and posters, "changed all the blackletter font into Futura", printed them and put them in an archive box. Her point is historical: the Nazi party itself banned blackletter in 1941. Whether the result reads as ironic, as the conversation assumed, is its reading and not hers.

Two books carry the joke on their covers. A print-on-demand [*Communist Manifesto (In Comic Sans)*](row:12.29) of 2020 calls the face "the most revolutionary" of fonts; the library that catalogued it describes "a meme not turned into, but being, a book itself". And in 2024 the British far-right activist Tommy Robinson published a book with [its title in Comic Sans](row:12.32). It went to number one on Amazon's British chart. A typeface that is supposed to make a text ridiculous did it no harm.

In Rome a school project of 2018, [Archigraphiæ](row:12.27), went the other way and studied the regime's own letters: students recorded the geometric lettering on Fascist buildings to show how small details turn "a neutral geometrical font into a very Italian-style fascist symbol".

## One word, one signature

Sometimes almost nothing is changed. In 1972 the Soviet artists Komar and Melamid [painted official slogans](row:12.02) in white on red, "OUR GOAL IS COMMUNISM!", and signed them with their own names. A signature turns an order into a quotation. Komar called it "self-cleansing" and, above all, "self-irony".

In 2000 Hans Haacke placed the words [DER BEVÖLKERUNG](row:12.10), to the population, in a courtyard of the Reichstag in Berlin, in the lettering of the inscription on its front, DEM DEUTSCHEN VOLKE, to the German people. Parliament let it stand by 260 votes to 258.

A German programme that helps people leave the far right, EXIT-Deutschland, [had 250 T-shirts](row:12.14) given away at a neo-Nazi rock festival in 2011. They said "Hardcore Rebellen". After one wash they said: "Was dein T-Shirt kann, kannst Du auch." What your T-shirt can do, you can do too.

## The page

A whole layout can be borrowed. Between 1978 and 1982 the Italian satirical weekly *Il Male* [printed counterfeit front pages](row:12.03) of the national dailies; one, during the funeral of the murdered statesman Aldo Moro, announced "Lo Stato si è estinto", the state is extinct. In 2008 the Yes Men and others handed out [a *New York Times*](row:12.12) dated the following year, headlined "IRAQ WAR ENDS", with the motto changed to "All the News We Hope to Print". The typography of authority is kept whole and carries other news.

Jenny Holzer did the reverse. For her [*Inflammatory Essays*](row:12.04) of 1979–82 she drew on Mao, Lenin, Emma Goldman, Hitler and Trotsky and pressed every text into one mould: a hundred words, twenty lines of italic capitals, a square of coloured paper, no name. The reader has to judge the words without the man.

The most thorough case is a scholarly one. When the copyright on *Mein Kampf* ran out, the Institute for Contemporary History in Munich published [an annotated edition](row:12.23), in January 2016. Hitler's text sits in a block on the right-hand page, and more than 3,500 notes surround it. The institute calls this "a kind of 'framing'" by which "a subtext to Mein Kampf is constructed". It runs to 1,948 pages, about twice the original.

A Japanese publisher's [manga version](row:12.11) of the same book, in 2008, sold 45,000 copies. The Bavarian finance ministry said it had "trouble considering manga as an appropriate medium".

# Colour, costume, voice

## Paint

On the night of 27 to 28 April 1991 the sculptor David Černý and friends [painted the Soviet tank monument](row:12.08) in Prague pink and set a raised finger on its turret. He was arrested and the tank repainted green. Fifteen members of parliament painted it pink again. Its protection as a monument was lifted and it was taken away. The monument did not survive a change of colour.

![A tank painted pink on a stone plinth.](../../../research_notes/Defusing%20by%20form/files/01_references/part-b/cerny-pink-tank-prague-1991.jpg)
The pink tank in Prague, 1991. Photograph by Meloun1212; CC0, Wikimedia Commons. [Row 12.08](row:12.08).

In Sofia, twenty years later, a group called Destructive Creation [painted the bronze soldiers](row:12.13) of the Soviet Army monument as Superman, Captain America and Ronald McDonald and wrote beneath them "in step with the times". It was cleaned off within four days. In Odesa in 2015 a Lenin condemned by the law on communist symbols [became Darth Vader](row:12.21): the head a helmet, the coat a cape, the clenched fist unchanged.

In Berlin since 2015 a graffiti artist, Ibo Omari, and other sprayers [have drawn over swastikas](row:12.18) on walls until they are "flowers, cars and animals", and published the templates.

## A turn, a foot

The smallest change in the collection is a rotation. The pink triangle with which the Nazis marked homosexuals pointed down. On [the poster "SILENCE = DEATH"](row:12.06) of 1987 it points up.

The painter Wang Guangyi [laid](row:12.07) the workers and Red Guards of Cultural Revolution posters over the logos of Coca-Cola and Chanel, altering neither. Kehinde Wiley's bronze [*Rumors of War*](row:12.28), set up in Richmond, Virginia, in 2019, copies the monument to a Confederate general with a young Black man in ripped jeans as the rider, and is "one foot taller".

## The voice

From 1996 the satirist Serdar Somuncu [toured Germany for years](row:12.09) reading *Mein Kampf* aloud on stage. The text was unchanged. What changed was the voice. Local officials tried to stop some shows, and neo-Nazi threats made him wear a bullet-proof vest.

In [*Hate Poetry*](row:12.16), from 2012 to 2015, German journalists of non-German background read out the racist letters their readers sent them, as a contest for the worst, with confetti. A critic explained why it works: "Die Journalisten nehmen ja die rassistischen Kommentare genau dadurch ernst, dass sie sie nicht zu ernst nehmen." The journalists take the racist comments seriously precisely by not taking them too seriously.

And in Wunsiedel in November 2014 the yearly neo-Nazi march [was turned](row:12.17), without the marchers' consent, into a sponsored walk: ten euros for every metre, paid to the programme that helps people leave. A table of bananas stood under a sign reading "Mein Mampf". The march raised 10,000 euros against itself.

Readers can do it too. A browser extension of 2016 [replaced](row:12.22) every picture of the American president-elect with a kitten and left the rest of the page alone. In January 2015 Japanese Twitter users [answered](row:12.19) a hostage video with tens of thousands of absurd collages of its stills, and were criticised for it while two lives were at stake.

# Where it fails, and what Bolzano has tried

## Four warnings

The collection includes its own objections.

The first is that ridicule is not owned by one side. The leaked style guide of a neo-Nazi website, [as a magazine excerpted it](row:12.26), tells its writers that most people are put off by "vitriolic, raging, nonironic hatred", and so: "The unindoctrinated should not be able to tell whether we are joking or not." The Comic Sans book cover belongs here too.

The second is that a new form can sell the old content. Charlotte Knobloch, a former president of the Central Council of Jews in Germany, [worried](row:12.24) that the annotated *Mein Kampf* would simply awaken interest in the original. The manga did sell.

The third is that irony may be what power counts on. The Slovene band Laibach and its circle [took the opposite road](row:12.05) from 1982: no distance at all. The philosopher Slavoj Žižek called it over-identification, a way of frustrating a system "precisely insofar as it is not its ironic imitation".

The fourth is that banning a joke spreads it. In 2017 Russia's justice ministry [listed](row:12.25) an image of Putin in clown's make-up as extremist material. The ban made it famous.

## In Italy and in Bolzano

Several of the works deal with what this library is about, the built remains of Fascism.

In Rome in 2009 Rossella Biscotti and Kevin van Braak [found five bronze heads](row:12.33) of Mussolini and the king in a storeroom, made for the world's fair of 1942 that never took place, and showed them for a week on freight pallets on a gallery floor. In 2022 Andrea Lo Giudice [made a project](row:12.30) in which single letters of Fascist inscriptions are coated with phosphorescent pigment, so that at night other words appear: on one wall, out of "NECESSARIO VINCERE" and "PIÙ NECESSARIO COMBATTERE", the letters of *amare*, to love. The same year the practice DAAR [built a scaled copy](row:12.36) of the front of a Fascist settlement in Sicily in fifteen pieces that people sit on to talk. Their word for it is to "profane" the architecture.

Three works are about Bolzano itself, and all three concern one relief and one monument.

In 2012 Francesco Arena made [*Trittico 57*](row:12.34) for Museion, the city's museum of modern and contemporary art. It is three slabs of travertine, of the size and stone of the three panels added in 1957 to complete the relief of Mussolini on horseback on the former party house. Into them he sandblasted, in Morse code, the letter with which the writer Italo Calvino left the Communist Party that same year.

In 2014 Gianni Pettena, who was born in Bolzano, [drew the Monument to Victory](row:12.35) as a ruin, its columns broken and the brush closing in. The series is called *Secoli e millenni*, centuries and millennia.

![A pencil drawing of a ruined monument among bushes, mountains behind.](../../../research_notes/Defusing%20by%20form/files/01_references/part-d/pettena_secoli_e_millenni_2014_monument_to_victory.jpg)

And in 2017 [a sentence of Hannah Arendt's](row:5.24) was set in lit letters across that relief, "Nobody has the right to obey", over the carved word "obbedire". It is the one work in this collection that answers a Fascist text in place, with a public authority behind it, and has stayed.

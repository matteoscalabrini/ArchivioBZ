Much of this research was done by programs that fetch pages and read them. Two of the libraries it needed most do not allow that. Teßmann digital, the newspaper archive of the provincial library in Bolzano, and the Italian state's general catalogue of cultural property both turn automated visitors away. A person with a browser gets in. On 22 October 2026 four questions the programs had left open were settled that way, by opening the pages by hand and reading them. This is what they gave.

## The bridge, as the regime's own paper described it

The first page was the *Alpenzeitung*, the regime's German-language daily, of [29 October 1931](row:3.14). It reports the day before, the ninth anniversary of the March on Rome, on which the Druso bridge was opened. The headline shows that the bridge was one item in a bundle: "Eröffnung von Bauwerken des Regimes", opening of works of the regime, namely the bridge, the municipal baths, the health-insurance building, a new transmitter for the state radio, a power station, a barracks and a tram line.

The ceremony was short. It rained. At nine in the morning the prefect arrived at the bridge, "wo er vom Erbauer Ing. Cav. Albini und dem technischen Stab empfangen wurde", where he was received by the builder, the engineer Albini, and the technical staff. A cut of the scissors, the tricolour ribbon fell, and the bridge was handed over to traffic. Albini is a new name. The histories give the engineers Miozzi and Ricci as the designers; he may have been the engineer on site.

Beside the report the paper printed a technical sheet. The bridge is of reinforced concrete faced in porphyry, 102.30 metres long, in three arches, with a roadway of 14 metres. Work began on 9 January 1930. It cost three million lire, of which the Comune paid 800,000. And the sheet describes the sculpture: on the corner piers four helmets with a Roman shield, and swords pointing up and down that "mark the beginning and the end of the war"; on the columns, Roman eagles on globes, the globes carried by the lictors' bundle on a base of laurel.

That answers a question the earlier notes had left open, whether the eagles were Roman decoration or Fascist emblem. By the regime's own description they were both, on purpose: the eagle of Rome standing on the bundle of the party.

## Two years later: who Drusus was

The second page is [the same paper on 26 September 1933](row:7.01), "Zwei Jahre Drusus-Brücke", two years of the Drusus bridge. It repeats the measurements and then explains the name: "Die Brücke trägt den Namen des römischen Feldherrn Drusus, der 15 Jahre vor Christi Geburt mit seinen Legionen unsere Alpengebiete eroberte, Straßen zog, Siedlungen gründete, römische Kultur in die von Sümpfen und Wildbächen durchtobten Täler trug." The bridge bears the name of the Roman general Drusus, who fifteen years before the birth of Christ conquered our Alpine lands with his legions, laid roads, founded settlements, and carried Roman culture into valleys ravaged by swamps and torrents.

The passage is the regime's claim to the province in one sentence, printed in German for German readers. The photograph beside it shows the eagle columns in place.

## Three posters in Treviso

The national poster collection, the Collezione Salce in Treviso, is catalogued in the state's general catalogue. The research on posters had known its Bolzano sheets only from the snippets of a search engine. Three records were now read in full.

Franz Lenhart's [*L'estate nelle Dolomiti*](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0500662577) carries the marks of the State Railways and of the state tourist board under the crowned arms of Savoy. It is the Italian edition of a poster that the tourism museum in Merano holds in German.

His [*Bozen-Gries Dolomiten*](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/HistoricOrArtisticProperty/0500658979), a man driving a car with a woman beside him, is the German edition of a poster known in English. The record adds a note of its own: Gries was joined to Bolzano in 1925, a scenic avenue replaced the dirt road between the two, the avenue was called Via del Littorio, and the poster advertises that road.

The third, *Visitate Bolzano-Gries*, has a printed signature, "J. Essendorfer / 1921". So the Italian call to "visit Bolzano-Gries" was on the walls in 1921, before Fascism was in power.

## 826,900 records for Rovigo

Many of the workers who came to Bolzano in the 1930s were from the province of Rovigo. To follow one of those families back, one needs the registers of births, marriages and deaths of its village, and the Italian archives' own portal has almost none for Rovigo after 1815. The research had been told they were on FamilySearch and could not get in to see.

By hand, [the collection](https://www.familysearch.org/en/search/collection/1482864) opens. It is called "Italia, Rovigo, Rovigo, Stato Civile (Tribunale), 1871-1937" and holds 826,900 records in 1,082,856 images, free to search by name. The same page confirms the collections for Treviso, up to 1941, and Venice, up to 1930.

## What changed

Four other texts in this library were corrected from these pages: the bridge got its starting date, its cost, a builder's name and its own paper's reading of the eagles; the radio got a new transmitter in 1931; three posters got their records; and the route for tracing a family got its most important stop confirmed.

One caution belongs with the first two pages. They are printed in the old German blackletter, and the quotations here were transcribed by eye, so small misreadings are possible.

# The state's print, 1916–1939

Much of what the Italian state did to make South Tyrol Italian was done with paper and letters: lists of names, decrees about how a name may be written, a law about the language of the first school class, forms for changing a surname. This text follows that printed apparatus in the order it appeared, and then the print that answered it from the German side.

## 1916: ten thousand names

The programme was written before Italy held the land. In 1916, in the middle of the war, Ettore Tolomei published in his journal the [*Prontuario dei nomi locali dell'Alto Adige*](row:16.01), a handbook of place names. Its introduction says what it is for. The state must make it evident that the new provinces belong to the country, "fin dal primo giorno dell'occupazione armata e civile", from the first day of armed and civil occupation, "con la nomenclatura dei luoghi, con tutto ciò ch'è scritto, stampato, pubblico ed apparente in pubblico": with the naming of places, with everything that is written, printed, public and visible in public.

The handbook offers, in its own count, "nei suoi 10.000 vocaboli, la toponomastica completa dell'Alto Adige". It is ranked by typeface. Ordinary type is for inhabited places, bold for the 204 communes, italic for mountains, valleys and waters. Each entry puts the German name first and the Italian one after it: "Bozen – Bolzano".

Five years later the border was staged for the whole country. The cover of the illustrated weekly *La Domenica del Corriere* for 23 October 1921 [shows](row:16.02) the king at the Brenner under the title "Il sacro rito del Brennero", the sacred rite of the Brenner, with his oath beneath: "questo Passo sarà eternamente nostro", this pass shall be ours for ever. That was a year before the Fascists took power.

## 1923: a decree on the size of letters

Fascism made the lists law. [The royal decree of 29 March 1923](row:16.03) fixed "la lezione ufficiale", the official reading, of the names of communes in the annexed territories. On the signs, stamps and seals of the state the Italian name was to be used. Some communes were allowed a second name beside it, and article 3 says how: "Il nome aggiunto nella seconda colonna deve tenere il secondo posto, fra parentesi, e non può essere scritto con caratteri più appariscenti di quelli del nome italiano." The added name must take second place, in brackets, and may not be written in characters more conspicuous than those of the Italian name.

In the list attached, Bolzano has no second name.

On the morning of Sunday 15 July 1923 Tolomei presented his whole programme in the municipal theatre of Bolzano. The German paper of the town [reported](row:16.04) the next day that he had spoken "über die Entnationalisierung Südtirols", on the denationalisation of South Tyrol. His measures included the Italianising of place names, of public inscriptions, of street names and of "Germanized surnames", and a ban on the very name "South Tyrol".

The ban reached the newspapers within weeks. The daily *Der Tiroler* had to drop its title in August and came out as *Der Landsmann*, the countryman.

## 1923: the school

On 1 October 1923 the school reform of the minister Gentile was signed, and one article dealt with the schools of the new provinces. ["A cominciare dall'anno scolastico 1923-24"](row:16.05), beginning with that school year, "in tutte le prime classi delle scuole elementari alloglotte l'insegnamento sarà impartito in lingua italiana": in all first classes of the elementary schools of other tongue, teaching shall be in Italian. The second class would follow a year later, and so on until every class was taught in Italian. The other language could be taught only "in ore aggiunte", in extra hours. Another article advised that Italian be taught by teachers who were not themselves of the other tongue.

The decree was published on 24 October. Three days later *Der Landsmann* [carried it](row:16.06) across its front page: "Die Italianisierung unserer Volksschulen". Under an appeal headed "Landsleute!", fellow countrymen, signed by the four German deputies, stands the sentence: "400 deutsche Schulen sind dem Untergange geweiht." Four hundred German schools are doomed.

German teaching went on in secret, and one document shows what that could cost. On 13 August 1926 the appointed mayor of San Leonardo in Passiria [wrote](row:16.08) to a Signora Ida Ladurner that he was keeping in force "la misura dell'espulsione da questo Comune", the measure of expulsion from this commune, "per avere Ella aperto … una scuola clandestina per l'insegnamento della lingua tedesca": because she had opened a clandestine school for the teaching of German. Since her daughter's injured foot had healed, she was to leave by 16 August. The letterhead is itself a record of that year, in which elected mayors were replaced by appointed ones: the printed words "IL SINDACO" are struck out, and "IL PODESTÀ" is stamped above them.

The podestà of San Leonardo in Passiria to Ida Ladurner, 13 August 1926, on her "scuola clandestina". Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0; the archive that holds it is not named. [Row 16.08](row:16.08).

The buildings kept their form and changed their names. The school named after the empress Elisabeth in Bolzano, occupied by Fascist squads on 1 October 1922, appears on [a postcard of 1928](row:16.10) as "Scuole Elementari Regina Elena".

The smallest children were reached through kindergartens. A national charity for the "redeemed" lands [ran about fifty](row:16.22) in the German-speaking districts by December 1927 and about a hundred by the end of the 1930s. The study of it takes its title from a sentence of the time: "L'asilo è il mezzo più potente per la penetrazione dell'italianità", the kindergarten is the most powerful means for the penetration of Italianness. In 1937 the authorities closed clandestine German kindergartens in a suburb of Bolzano. In 1940 many of the charity's own kindergartens closed for lack of children. The families had opted for Germany.

## 1926–1935: surnames and a gravestone

From 1926 surnames could be "given back" their Italian form. A decree of January that year made it law for the province of Trento, to which Bolzano then belonged, and the prefects carried it out name by name.

One issue of the official gazette, [of 5 October 1935](row:16.14), is filled for page after page with decrees of the prefect of Bolzano. Each follows the same form. A request has been presented; it has been posted for a month at the town hall; no one has objected; "la restituzione del cognome in forma italiana" is granted and extended to wife and children. On one page, dated 26 May 1935, Egger becomes Mancini, Oberkofler becomes Covoli, Castlunger becomes Costalunga, Feichtinger becomes Fedrighi. On paper each is a request.

![A page of the official gazette listing decrees, each with an old and a new surname.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/16_propaganda_print/images/1935-10-05_GU_n233_Bolzano_cognomi_p4843.jpg)
*Gazzetta Ufficiale*, 5 October 1935, p. 4843: decrees of the prefect of Bolzano "restoring" surnames to an Italian form. Official act. [Row 16.14](row:16.14).

In the churchyard of Gargazzone a stone of the same year [reads](row:16.13): "Qui riposa Luigi Gruber ex Sindaco di Vanga nato 1865 morto 8.2.1935." Here lies Luigi Gruber, former mayor of Vanga. He was Alois Gruber, mayor of Wangen. No rule that forced German off gravestones has been found in a primary text. The stone shows at least what had become usual.

In 1938 even a learned journal changed its name. *Der Schlern*, a monthly of local history named after the mountain above Bolzano, [came out](row:16.15) in July as *Der Sciliar*: the Italian name of the mountain, set in German blackletter.

## 1939: the regime's own magazine

In March 1939 the prefecture gave the province a magazine of its own, [*Atesia Augusta*](row:16.16). The prefect chaired its board and the party's press cooperative published it. Its title is set in Roman capitals with V for U, AVGVSTA, and the price on the back cover reads "LIRE QVATTRO". The first issue opens with a portrait of Mussolini and a dedication in his hand, which, as far as it can be read, is to the review "che ha nel nome il programma", which has its programme in its name. The back cover is the Monument to Victory.

Inside are Tolomei on "Terra d'Italia", an article on the Roman character of the Alto Adige, another on the dictionary of place names being compiled "casale per casale, malga per malga", farmstead by farmstead and alp by alp, aerial photographs of the new quarters, and in every issue a section headed "Vita del Partito".

![The cover of a magazine with a tower, a fir tree and mountain peaks.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/16_propaganda_print/images/1939-03_AtesiaAugusta_n1_cover_BPI.jpg)
*Atesia Augusta*, year I, no. 1, March 1939; cover by Guido Pelizzari. Biblioteca Provinciale Italiana Claudia Augusta; rights not stated. [Row 16.16](row:16.16).

The first year of the magazine runs straight across the Option. The March issue presents a Roman and Italian province. The November issue has an unsigned article on "L'esecuzione degli accordi italo-germanici per i Tedeschi dell'Alto Adige", the carrying out of the Italo-German agreements for the Germans of the Alto Adige. The issues in between could not be seen.

# The answer, 1923–1948

## 1923–1932: appeals, a book, two pamphlets

The first answer was the deputies' appeal of October 1923, on a front page.

In 1928 one of those deputies, Eduard Reut-Nicolussi, published *Tirol unterm Beil*, Tyrol under the axe. [Its cover](row:16.09) turns the regime's emblem against it: a red bundle of rods with its axe hangs over a dark town and mountains.

![A book cover: a red fasces with an axe above a dark town and mountains.](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/16_propaganda_print/images/1928_ReutNicolussi_Tirol_unterm_Beil_cover_Commons.jpg)
Eduard Reut-Nicolussi, *Tirol unterm Beil*, cover of the popular edition, dated 1928 on Wikimedia Commons; CC BY-SA 4.0. [Row 16.09](row:16.09).

The Fascist paper in Bolzano kept up a campaign against the Andreas-Hofer-Bund, an association abroad that campaigned for South Tyrol. A search of its text finds the name in 1926, 1927, 1928 and 1929, always as an enemy.

The most consequential pamphlet came from an unexpected side. In 1926 Hitler published [*Die Südtiroler Frage und das Deutsche Bündnisproblem*](row:16.07), the South Tyrol question and the problem of Germany's alliances. It gave the province up for the sake of an alliance with Italy. Six years later a Munich pamphlet [attacked him for it](row:17.06) under a title that speaks of German South Tyrol "in Schmerz und Schand", in pain and shame. It is the piece of anti-Nazi print in this library, and a reproduction of its cover hangs today in the exhibition under the Monument to Victory.

## 1939: the Option on paper

When the choice between Germany and Italy was put to the population in 1939, both sides printed, typed and painted.

Those who wanted people to stay had little more than a typewriter. An anonymous leaflet headed ["Landsleute!"](row:16.17) warns that the German government has proposed "die Südtiroler Deutschen aus dem Lande ihrer Väter abzuschieben und nach Galizien zu verpflanzen", to deport the South Tyrolean Germans from the land of their fathers and transplant them to Galicia. "Für Galizien? 'Kennt Ihr das Land?'" For Galicia? Do you know the land? The question is Goethe's line about Italy, which a tourist poster had used for Merano a few years before. Here it is turned on the promised new home. The leaflet ends: "gebt keine Unterschrift her!" Give no signature. At its foot it names the "sizilianische Gespenst", the Sicilian spectre: the rumour that those who stayed would be shipped south.

![A typewritten leaflet headed Landsleute!](../../../research_notes/Fascist%20Bolzano%20media%20and%20migration/files/16_propaganda_print/images/1939_Option_Flugblatt_Landsleute_Commons.jpg)
"Landsleute!", anonymous typewritten leaflet against emigration, autumn 1939. Wikimedia Commons; public domain according to Commons, its status under discussion there. [Row 16.17](row:16.17).

The other side is represented by two posters painted by hand. One shows a family in costume, the woman lifting a child towards a sun with a swastika rising over the mountains, under the words ["Heim ins Reich!"](row:16.18) in black blackletter, home into the Reich. The other, signed "Klause", [reads](row:16.19) "Der Führer ruft! Heim ins Reich!" Where they were made and where they are kept is not known; the person who put them online names no source, and the first is contested there.

The Italian state filed the result on orange cards. [One survives](row:16.20) online with the personal details blacked out: "Scheda individuale – Schedario degli allogeni tedeschi che hanno optato per la Germania", individual card, index of the German allogeni who have opted for Germany. The word for the people, *allogeni*, is of one family with the school decree's word for their schools, *alloglotte*. On the back a stamp of 8 April 1948 records that the same person asked to take the choice back. The card was in use across the fall of the regime.

The series ends with a postcard of 1941–42 by the painter Albert Stolz for the organisation of those who had opted, [*Heimat und Front*](row:16.21): on one side a farm woman with a hoe and two children, on the other soldiers throwing a grenade, between them the party eagle.

## Still open

- Tolomei's programme of 1923 in its own Italian text. It is known here from a German newspaper's summary and a later translation.
- The issues of *Atesia Augusta* between March and December 1939, to see how the Option was told to Italians month by month.
- A primary source for the often-repeated ban on German gravestone inscriptions.
- A schoolbook or exercise book of the time that shows the Brenner or Bolzano.
- Where the two "Heim ins Reich" posters come from, and a printed leaflet from the side that campaigned for leaving.
- What the rite at the Brenner in October 1921 was.

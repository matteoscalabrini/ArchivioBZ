# Arrivals, 1934–1943

In the ten years before 1943 two movements of people crossed in Bolzano. Italians from the Veneto, Trentino, Lombardy and Piedmont came north to the new factories. South Tyroleans who had opted for Germany went north in their turn, out of the country. This text follows both, with the numbers as far as they hold, and then asks a practical question: how could one family be followed by name?

## 1934–1936: the zone on paper

The industrial zone began as law. [A decree of 28 September 1934](row:10.02) promised firms that would build in Bolzano freedom from customs duties on their machinery, ten years free of the tax on their income, and operating grants. [A second decree](row:10.03), of 7 March 1935, let the Comune expropriate the land "compresa fra il fiume Isarco e la strada nazionale del Brennero", between the Isarco and the Brenner road, [about 300 hectares](row:4.08). The first plots went to Lancia, the steelworks, Montecatini, Masonite and Magnesio that same year.

The papers of the office that ran the zone are in the city archive, [from 1935 to the early 1980s](row:4.28): the purchases and expropriations, the owners' lawsuits for more compensation, the firms' requests for plots, the roads, rails and sewers.

## 1937–1942: the plants and the people

The first firm to produce was a foundry, [in February 1937](https://www.provinz.bz.it/cultura/manifestazioni_old/archivio/mostra_foto/volumi/miori_index.htm). Montecatini's aluminium works [started](row:4.11) on 29 April 1937, the first shop of Lancia the same year, the steelworks of the Falck group and Masonite in 1938.

How many worked there is known only in snapshots. On 31 October 1939 [a local history counts](https://www.provinz.bz.it/cultura/manifestazioni_old/archivio/mostra_foto/volumi/miori_index.htm) "20 ditte che davano lavoro a 3.004 operai", twenty firms giving work to 3,004 workers: 900 at the aluminium works, 650 at the steelworks, 600 at Lancia, 250 at Masonite. By 1942 there were "quasi settemila operai", almost seven thousand, [according to the historian Carlo Romeo](https://www.novecento.org/sul-confine-lalto-adige-sudtirol-nella-storia-del-novecento/il-fascismo-al-brennero-un-breve-profilo-per-questioni-7361/).

The newcomers were sorted by skill. Romeo again: "I tecnici e gli operai specializzati provenivano in maggioranza dalle case madri lombarde e piemontesi, mentre la manovalanza soprattutto dal Veneto e dal Trentino." The technicians and skilled workers came mostly from the parent firms in Lombardy and Piedmont, the labourers above all from the Veneto and Trentino. Another historian, Stefano Gallo, [names the provinces](https://www.asei.eu/it/2017/05/operazioni-migratorie-belliche-transfrontaliere-e-rimpatri-da-una-parte-allaltra-delle-alpi/): families "provenienti soprattutto dalle province di Padova, Verona e Rovigo". Lancia brought men from Turin and in January 1942 planned 32 wooden huts for them.

Local people were mostly left out. Romeo reports that the grants to the firms were awarded partly by the number of their workers who came from outside the province, labour "regnicola e non allogena", from the old kingdom and not of other stock, as the awarding commission put it in 1936. The phrase is the commission's and not the decree's. A statistic for 1939 fits it: industry and crafts employed 14.6 per cent of the German speakers who were at work and 23 per cent of the Italians.

From April 1937 an office of the regime's commissariat for internal migration sat in Bolzano and [checked the transfers](https://www.asei.eu/it/2017/05/operazioni-migratorie-belliche-transfrontaliere-e-rimpatri-da-una-parte-allaltra-delle-alpi/) of workers from other provinces, in agreement with the Fascist party federation.

## The count

The city's register gives arrivals from Italian provinces into the commune of Bolzano year by year: 7,655 in 1937, 4,203 in 1938, 5,773 in 1939, 6,425 in 1940.

The censuses and the register show the result. Bolzano had [32,679 inhabitants](row:4.01) in 1921 and 45,505 in 1936. The municipal register counts 56,812 on 1 January 1939 and 64,494 on 1 January 1943. In 1951 the census found 70,898.

By language the picture is rougher, because no census between 1931 and 1951 asked the question. For the province, [one reconstruction](https://www.asei.eu/it/2006/11/limmigrazione-italiana-in-alto-adige-approcci-e-questioni/) puts Italian speakers at 6,950 in 1910, three per cent; 20,306 in 1921; 80,743 in 1939, a quarter; and 104,766 in 1943, more than a third. The inflow from the rest of Italy between 1921 and 1939 is estimated at 55,000 to 56,000 people.

No source found says what share of the newcomers came from which province. A small sample exists by accident. On 3 May 1945, in the last days of the war, 41 people were killed in Bolzano, more than twenty of them at the wall of the Lancia works. [The record of that day](row:4.21), in an atlas of Nazi and Fascist massacres, gives each victim's place of origin from the city's register. Five came from the province of Padua, five from Verona, three from Rovigo, six from Trentino, four from South Tyrol, and the rest singly from places between Aosta and Cosenza.

Where they lived is the subject of another text in this library, on the Semirurali. One voice from there will do here. "Mio padre è venuto in bicicletta dal Cadore", a former resident [told the quarter's museum](row:4.20): my father came by bicycle from the Cadore. "Ha trovato lavoro alla Lancia e in poco tempo ha ricevuto anche l'appartamento." He found work at Lancia and soon got the flat as well.

# Departures: the Option, 1939–1955

## 1939: a choice with a deadline

The Option was never a signed treaty. It came out of [a conference in Berlin](https://www.tirol.gv.at/kunst-kultur/landesarchiv/archivglossar/archivglossar-o/) on 23 June 1939, "ohne dass eine förmliche Vereinbarung geschlossen worden wäre", without a formal agreement being concluded. Guidelines of 21 October 1939 fixed the procedure, and every German-speaking and Ladin South Tyrolean had to hand in a binding declaration by 31 December: German citizenship and emigration to the Reich, or Italian citizenship.

A rumour did part of the work. Those who chose Italy, it said, would be moved to Sicily, or to Abyssinia, or at least south of the Po. Leaflets of the VKS, the Nazi movement of South Tyrol, spread it, and on 6 July 1939 the prefect himself [had spoken](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sizilianische_Legende) of 20,000 people who would have to emigrate or be resettled south of the Po. Historians call it the Sicilian legend. Whether the German consul general in Milan launched it on purpose is disputed.

The German side had its office in place early. The agency for emigration, under Himmler, [began work](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtliche_deutsche_Ein-_und_R%C3%BCckwanderungsstelle) on 15 September 1939 with its headquarters in the Hotel Bristol in Bolzano and branches in Merano, Bressanone, Brunico and Vipiteno.

## The numbers, and why there are several

Three figures circulate for the result, and they are counts of different things.

The official German-Italian statistic of 1940, "die allerdings nicht sehr verlässlich ist", which is admittedly not very reliable, in the words of the Tyrolean state archive, counted about 267,000 people entitled to opt: 69.4 per cent for Germany, 11.9 per cent expressly for Italy, and 18.7 per cent who handed in nothing and so stayed Italian. The well-known 86 per cent is a modern recalculation for the territory of today's province, with about 246,000 entitled. And the two totals often quoted for the province of the time, about 166,000 and about 213,000, are one result counted twice: heads of household signed for their wives and under-age children.

No figure was found for the city of Bolzano alone.

## 1940–1943: who went

Opting was not leaving. By 1 July 1942 there were 222,018 optants for Germany, of whom 76,824 [had gone](https://www.tirol.gv.at/kunst-kultur/landesarchiv/archivglossar/archivglossar-u/). By the end of 1943 the official total was 81,500. The archive in Innsbruck adds that the true number of civilians was probably between 60,000 and 70,000, since some 25,000 South Tyrolean men were drafted into German service. The figure usually given is about 75,000. The historian who leads the digitising of the files [said in 2026](row:4.35): "Wir können bis heute nicht genau sagen, wie viele Menschen letztendlich Südtirol im Zuge der Option verlassen haben." To this day we cannot say exactly how many people finally left South Tyrol in the course of the Option.

Their property was to pass into Italian hands. [A law of 27 November 1939](row:10.08) turned an older land agency into the Ente nazionale per le Tre Venezie, with the task, [as a historian summarises it](row:4.18), "di prendere in consegna i beni immobili degli optanti per poi rivenderli", of taking over the optants' real estate and selling it on. Within two years it held farms and land and also houses, flats, hotels, shops and offices. How much it bought, and where the Italian settlers were to come from, could not be found for this research. The book that should have the figures was out of reach.

So in the same years the two movements passed each other: between four and eight thousand Italians a year arriving in the commune of Bolzano, and some 75,000 South Tyroleans leaving the province.

## After 1945: coming back

A decree of 2 February 1948 allowed the options to be revised. How many came back is as uncertain as how many left. The archive in Innsbruck estimates 8,000 to 12,000 illegal returns and about 9,000 legal ones between 1949 and 1955. Historians' totals run from 15,000 to 25,000.

One of those families ended up in the workers' quarter built for the Italians. "1949, im Oktober, sind wir von Graz als Rückoptanten nach Bozen gekommen", they [told the Semirurali museum](row:4.20). In October 1949 we came from Graz to Bolzano as returning optants. "Es war eine große Errungenschaft die Wohnung in der via Bari." The flat in via Bari was a great achievement.

The land agency outlived the regime. In 1949 it was [still leasing](row:4.04) 52 farms of 12 to 20 hectares in the Adige valley, year by year, to tenants "provenienti da diverse zone", from various areas. It lasted into the 1970s, when its property passed to the Province.

# Following one family by name

## Two ways of being on file

The two groups left very different traces.

Those who opted for Germany were carded one by one, and the cards are now online. The University of Innsbruck's database OptionDigital holds 113,379 resettlement cards from the Tyrolean state archive and 24,936 cards of those who applied to come back, from the state archive in Bolzano, with scans of both sides. A card gives name, date and place of birth, religion, trade, wife and children, the address in South Tyrol and a file number under which the personal file can be ordered in Innsbruck. The stamps on the back say what happened next. [A sheet in this library](row:4.34) explains how to read them.

For the Italians who arrived, nothing of the kind exists. Their move is recorded in the population register of the commune of Bolzano, which is not online. The trace has to start there and run backwards.

## From Bolzano back to the Polesine

Take a family that came from the province of Rovigo, the Polesine, to the Semirurali in 1938. This research worked out the route and tested each stop, though it has not yet been walked for a real family.

The first stop is the registry office of Bolzano, which issues historical certificates of residence and of family composition for a past date. They should give the day the family was entered and the commune it came from. Births, marriages and deaths in Bolzano are registered from 1924, when civil registration began in South Tyrol.

The second is the lists of names that Bolzano's own history produced. The police headquarters kept [a register of "subversives"](row:4.03) around 1930, many of them railwaymen. The state archive has posted lists of soldiers who came back from German internment in 1945. And there is the camp.

From 1944 to 1945 the German occupiers ran a police transit camp in Bolzano, from which thirteen transports left for Mauthausen, Flossenbürg, Dachau, Ravensbrück and Auschwitz. The Comune publishes [a list of 8,294 names](row:4.32) of its prisoners. Dario Venegoni's [book](row:4.13) has 7,982 entries with birthplace, trade, arrest, camp number and fate. The totals given for the camp are 9,500 or 11,000, so even here between one and three thousand people have no name. Among those who have one are workers of the zone. Romeo Trevisan, a mechanic born in Padua in 1915, worked at Lancia and led the resistance cell in the factory. He was arrested in Bolzano on 19 December 1944, deported to Mauthausen on 1 February 1945, and died at Gusen on 29 March. Prisoners were also put to forced labour in the zone's factories, and people of the Semirurali, by one account in the same book, smuggled food and letters to them.

The third stop is the army. A father born in the Polesine was called up there, and his papers are in the state archives of Padua and Rovigo. A son who turned eighteen in Bolzano is probably in the conscription lists of Bolzano. None of this is online.

The fourth is the birthplace. Here the usual advice fails. The Italian state archives' portal for family history, Antenati, has [almost nothing](row:4.36) for the province of Rovigo after 1815: 690 registers in all, 30 of them from after unification. The records are on FamilySearch, as "Italia, Rovigo, Rovigo, Stato Civile (Tribunale), 1871-1937", which a check by hand for this research found to hold 826,900 records. The state archive of Rovigo [says the same](row:4.23) in its own guide. Verona and Mantua, by contrast, are on Antenati almost complete, and Padua, Venice and Treviso are again on FamilySearch.

Before 1871 the trail goes to the parishes and the diocese of Adria-Rovigo, and for 1806 to 1815 back to Antenati.

## Names already in print

A few people who made this journey can be named from published sources. The oral-history archive of the Italian provincial library in Bolzano, [described in a recent thesis](row:4.24), has an interview with Mario Dall'Aglio, born at Castelguglielmo in 1927, who arrived in 1937 and later lived in via Belluno in the Semirurali, and one with Luigi Chieregato from Fratta Polesine, who came in 1940.

The record of 3 May 1945 names three men from the Polesine among the dead: Walter Saudo of Badia Polesine, a carpenter of 59 and a partisan; Vittorio Giuriola of Costa di Rovigo, a worker of 42; Sergio Laghi of Polesella, who was 23.

## Still open

- The share of each province among the newcomers of 1934–43. The city's register would give it.
- The figures of the Option for the city of Bolzano: how many opted, left and returned.
- What the land agency bought by 1943, and whether there was a plan for where its settlers should come from.
- Where the housing institute's files on who was given which house are kept, if they survive.
- The route above, walked once for a real family.
